# Squeezing Federated LLMs Onto Weak Devices

Between 2024 and 2026, federated LLM work for low-resource and resource-heterogeneous clients split into five strategies, and **no single method wins on accuracy, memory, energy and latency together on truly low-end hardware**. Backprop-free tuning (zeroth-order or "forward-only": FwdLLM, FedKSeed, FedMeZO, FedSPZO, Meerkat) brings client memory down to roughly inference level and communication down to **under 18 KB per round**. It pays with many forward passes per update and an accuracy gap that its own authors admit to. First-order PEFT keeps that accuracy by shrinking or moving the problem: a 4-bit backbone (HAFLQ, FedQuad, the FlowerTune 4-bit LoRA recipe), per-client layer subsets (FedRA, FedBiOT), or a server that runs the backward pass (MobiLLM, Fed MobiLLM, split FL). Heterogeneous-rank LoRA has become a crowded sub-field of its own. Its aggregation rules are moving from zero-padding (HetLoRA) toward product-space aggregation in which the rank a client communicates is decoupled from the rank it trains locally (FlexLoRA → FSLoRA → FedPA-LoRA). For clients with roughly 4 GB of RAM or less, the most realistic design keeps only a small language model (SLM) on the device and a large one on the server, linked by mutual distillation (FedMKT, FedCoLLM, FedProxy). Rank heterogeneity only helps clients that can already hold the shared backbone. On the inference side, 4-bit weight-only quantization plus NPU-aware engines make 0.1–3B models practical on phones and boards. Natively ternary models (BitNet b1.58 2B4T, reportedly about 0.4 GB) are the most important 2025 step for CPU-only devices. Microcontroller-class work still stops at BERT-scale encoders and 8–30M-parameter "micro-LMs". The largest blind spot is energy. The notes found **no peer-reviewed wall-plug measurement of federated LLM fine-tuning on a phone, Raspberry Pi, Jetson or MCU**, and real-device training evidence tops out at about 1.3B parameters. Most claims of "low-resource feasibility" rest on GPU simulation with memory accounting, so the evidence below should be read with that in mind.

## How to read the evidence in this report

The underlying research could not open arXiv full texts, because the domain was blocked in the research environment, and the shared search budget ran out. **Every number here is the authors' own claim as it appeared in a search-result snippet, abstract or HTML summary. None was checked against the PDF**, and the notes contain no independent replications. Each table carries an evidence tag. **S** means the primary paper's abstract or snippet. **2°** means a secondary source, such as a citing paper, a review site or a blog. **[PK]** means the researchers' prior knowledge: the arXiv ID or venue was not re-verified, so check it before you cite it. **T** means only the title or a reference-list entry was seen and the contents are unverified. "n/v" in a code column means no repository was verified. Venue labels such as "KDD 2024 [PK]" mean the arXiv preprint was confirmed but the venue was not.

## Weights and activations set the memory floor, and each family attacks a different term

The most useful way to compare these methods is to ask which term of device memory each one removes. Device memory during fine-tuning is the sum of frozen weights, activations stored for backprop, optimizer state and trainable parameters. Zeroth-order and side-tuning methods remove the activation and optimizer terms but leave the weights resident. Quantized LoRA shrinks the weights but keeps backprop activations. Layer-allocation and emulator methods shrink everything by giving the client a smaller model, at an accuracy cost. Distillation removes the large model from the device entirely. This is why the researchers concluded that **running federated fine-tuning on 2–4 GB devices needs zeroth-order or side-tuning combined with a backbone quantized to 4 bits or fewer**. No single technique gets there alone ([FwdLLM](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc24/presentation/xu-mengwei); [Fed MobiLLM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06765); [HAFLQ](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06581v2)). The edge-device notes reach the same view from the systems side. Activation memory, not FLOPs, is the binding constraint, and that is why all three dominant 2025 designs (forward-gradient, side-tuning, split learning) avoid storing activations on the device ([Fed MobiLLM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06765)).

The table also shows that **"low-resource" means three different things in this literature**: low memory (zeroth-order, quantization, side-tuning), low bandwidth (seed-sharing, sparse zeroth-order, distillation) and low energy. Almost no paper measures the third directly.

## Backprop-free tuning reaches inference-level memory but pays in forward passes

Zeroth-order (ZO) federated tuning estimates gradients from perturbed forward passes, so a client that can run inference, including on an inference-only NPU, can also train. **FwdLLM (USENIX ATC 2024) reported up to three orders of magnitude faster convergence than earlier backprop-free baselines and a 4.6× lower memory footprint.** Its edge-device results ran gradient estimation on mobile NPUs, which it reports as **19.5–27.5× faster than CPU backprop** ([USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc24/presentation/xu-mengwei); [arXiv 2308.13894](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.13894)). Its 2026 journal extension, FwdLLM+, uses low-rank perturbations and claims up to **151× faster training and 93% less memory than vanilla backprop-based federated LLM tuning** ([IEEE TMC](https://www.computer.org/csdl/journal/tm/2026/08/11429542/2eOD933hNSw)). FedKSeed (ICML 2024) attacked communication instead. Clients share only (seed, scalar-gradient) pairs drawn from a finite pool of K seeds, so full-parameter tuning of billion-scale models costs **under 18 KB per round** ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/qin24a.html)). FedKSeed argues that FwdLLM can need several hundred GB of communication for a model of about 300M parameters. That is a competitor's claim and should be read as such ([arXiv 2312.06353](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.06353v2)). Surveys also disagree on where FedKSeed belongs: one files it under black-box or inference-only prompt tuning ([arXiv 2508.16261](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.16261)).

The literature's direction is easy to read from the sequence of papers. FedMeZO (2024) established that ZO federated tuning converges ([arXiv 2402.05926](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05926v3)). The 2025 papers attack sample efficiency and non-IID drift: FedSPZO varies the number of perturbations per block, and Meerkat restricts updates to a transferable, very sparse parameter subset ([arXiv 2502.10239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10239); [arXiv 2506.03337](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03337v1)). The 2026 papers add stabilization and hybridization: adaptive momentum (ACL 2026), heterogeneous block activation (ZorBA), and cloud-side first-order correction of on-device ZO updates (CooperLLM) ([ACL 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1851.pdf); [arXiv 2603.04436](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.04436); [arXiv 2601.12917](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.12917)). The move toward cloud correction is telling. The researchers read it as a sign that **pure on-device ZO is still too slow or inaccurate to stand alone**. FedSPZO's authors concede that ZO still trails first-order LoRA ([arXiv 2502.10239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10239)), and a competing paper notes that ZO's multiple forward passes per update raise on-device compute ([Fed MobiLLM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06765)). FedKSeed also warns that an overly large learning rate can cause divergence ([arXiv 2312.06353](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.06353)).

The researchers drew one inference from this that matters for low-resource clients. ZO is memory-optimal only with respect to activations and optimizer state, so a client still needs the whole backbone in RAM. For billion-scale models on 2–4 GB devices, that means ZO has to run against a quantized backbone. **The notes found no strong 2025–2026 evidence of ZO federated tuning on 2-bit or ternary backbones.**

## Quantized, partial and server-assisted tuning keep first-order accuracy by moving the problem

The first-order alternatives keep backprop and change what the client holds. **4-bit QLoRA has become the de facto baseline.** A public FlowerTune leaderboard entry fine-tunes Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with 4-bit quantization, LoRA rank 8 and FedProx, simulated on a single GPU ([Flower app](https://flower.ai/apps/mrs83/flowertune_qwen2.5-7b-instruct-medical)). Research variants push further. HAFLQ applies salience-driven, block-wise adaptive quantization to the frozen backbone and combines it with heterogeneous ranks. It reports **31% less memory**, but only on text classification, as the authors themselves note ([arXiv 2411.06581](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06581v2)). FedQuad adapts LoRA depth per device and **quantizes activations**, which on small devices often cost more memory than the weights ([arXiv 2506.01001](https://export.arxiv.org/abs/2506.01001)). FedPipe quantizes the base model differently for each edge server so that it fits and assigns per-server LoRA ranks and batch sizes through an MILP solved heuristically ([arXiv 2404.06448](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.06448v2)). A privacy-motivated variant ships a quantized backbone and LoRA to clients to cut download cost and protect the server's IP ([arXiv 2406.10976](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.10976v1)).

Layer allocation hands each client a smaller model. FedRA (ECCV 2024) gives each client a random subset of layers per round, sized to its capacity. It works even when **no client can hold the full model**, and every layer still learns from many clients ([arXiv 2311.11227](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11227)). FedBiOT (KDD 2024) and the offsite-tuning family (FedOT, FATE-LLM) send clients a compressed "emulator" plus a trainable adapter, which protects model IP ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=i5da6iedW8); [survey arXiv 2508.16261](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.16261)). The cost is accuracy. The 2026 FedProxy paper reports that offsite-tuning methods **consistently underperform centralized fine-tuning** and proposes proxy SLMs instead ([arXiv 2604.19015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.19015)).

Server-assisted designs remove backprop from the device altogether. MobiLLM keeps the frozen backbone on the phone and offloads backprop of a trainable side network to a server. It reports **4.487 GB of device memory for OPT-1.3B**, which is about the only absolute device-memory figure for LLM fine-tuning in the notes ([arXiv 2502.20421](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20421)). Fed MobiLLM makes this federated and **has the best real-hardware evidence in the area: Jetson TX2, Xavier NX and AGX Xavier with OPT-125M to 1.3B**. It still uses boards rather than phones ([arXiv 2508.06765](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06765v1)). Split federated learning cuts device memory further but moves the bottleneck to the uplink, because activations and gradients cross the link every step. That is why the wireless papers jointly optimize split point, LoRA rank and radio resources ([FedsLLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14667v2)). Only one 2026 split-FL study runs on real phones, with three Huawei nova 9 Pro handsets among five clients ([Just Talk Once](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.01457)). The edge notes also raise a privacy problem that these designs leave open: uploaded activations can leak inputs, and **none of the split-FL papers in the snippets offered formal privacy guarantees**.

## Heterogeneous LoRA ranks became a sub-field with a settled problem and unsettled answers

The core difficulty is that the average of LoRA products is not the product of averaged factors. Naive FedAvg on A and B therefore injects aggregation noise, and heterogeneous ranks make the noise worse ([FLoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.05976); [FedEx-LoRA](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.67/)). Four families of fix have emerged, each with a known cost. **Zero-pad and truncate** (HetLoRA, EMNLP 2024) is cheap but biased: Byun et al. (NAACL 2025) show that padding dilutes the information carried by high-rank clients, and the 2026 raFLoRA paper links it to rank collapse ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.717); [arXiv 2406.17477](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17477); [arXiv 2602.13486](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13486)). **SVD redistribution of the full product** (FlexLoRA, NeurIPS 2024) addresses the "buckets effect", in which the weakest clients hold everyone back. It reports **+2.14% on average and up to +2.72%**, at the cost of server-side SVD ([arXiv 2402.11505](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11505)). **Stacking** (FLoRA, NeurIPS 2024) is exact, but the global rank equals the sum of client ranks, so adapter size and download cost grow with the number of clients ([NeurIPS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/28312c9491d60ed0c77f7fff4ad86dd1-Paper-Conference.pdf)). **Sketching, product-space and dimension-wise** schemes from 2025–2026 (FSLoRA, FedPA-LoRA, FediLoRA, PreLort, SeFoRA, Fed-PLoRA) try to get exactness without that growth. FedPA-LoRA explicitly **decouples local compute rank from communication rank**, which suits bandwidth-limited clients ([arXiv 2608.15381](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.15381)).

The evidence on which approach wins conflicts. FSLoRA's own head-to-head numbers favour FlexLoRA over FLoRA, at **77.90 vs. 72.81** under a normal rank distribution and **75.59 vs. 73.43** under a heavy-tailed one ([arXiv 2501.19389](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19389v4)). LoRA-FAIR reports that FLoRA failed to converge in its setup, which it attributes to FLoRA adding updates directly to the base model ([arXiv 2411.14961](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.14961)). The notes found **no neutral benchmark that compares these methods under measured device memory rather than nominal rank**. The point matters for low-resource clients. Rank changes adapter memory, which is small, but leaves the frozen backbone and activations untouched, and those dominate. The researchers' conclusion follows: **rank heterogeneity only helps clients that can already hold the backbone**. For weaker clients, it is the wrong tool.

## Distillation lets the weakest clients hold only a small model

When a client cannot hold the backbone even at 4 bits, the remaining route is to never send it. **FedMKT (COLING 2025)** pairs a server LLM with client SLMs that can differ in architecture and tokenizer. Tokens are matched by minimum-edit-distance alignment, the two sides distill selectively in both directions on a public dataset, and **both the LLM and the SLMs improve**. The server LLM reaches performance comparable to fine-tuning directly on client data ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.17/)). FedCoLLM keeps client work to LoRA on the SLM and runs mutual distillation on the server ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/385920407_FedCoLLM_A_Parameter-Efficient_Federated_Co-tuning_Framework_for_Large_and_Small_Language_Models)). FedProxy (2026) generalizes this to proxy SLMs with heterogeneity-aware fusion ([arXiv 2604.19015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.19015)). The weakness is the public dataset. FLoKD (Sep 2026) criticises FedMKT for using public data without judging its relevance, and a 2026 reasoning-distillation paper allocates data by learnability ([arXiv 2609.13580](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.13580); [arXiv 2602.18749](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18749)). Logit-based distillation also saves bandwidth, cutting inter-client communication by **roughly 50%** in one wireless study ([arXiv 2509.01750](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01750v2)).

Production systems already work this way. Google trains small Gboard LMs with federated learning plus differential privacy. Since 2025 it has used DP-FL signals from devices to steer LLM-generated synthetic data that fine-tunes the server-side Gboard Proofread LLM ([Google Research blog](https://www.research.google/blog/synthetic-and-federated-privacy-preserving-domain-adaptation-with-llms-for-mobile-applications/), snippet only). Earlier, pretraining on LLM-synthesized data gave **19.0% and 22.8% relative next-word-prediction gains** over a model trained on a standard public dataset ([arXiv 2404.04360](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.04360)). The notes found no shipped system that runs gradient training of an LLM on the device.

## Ternary weights and NPU engines make on-device inference practical, while MCUs stop near 30M parameters

For 0.1–3B models, on-device inference is mature on phones, Pis and Jetson boards. The standard stack is 4-bit weight-only quantization (AWQ, llama.cpp GGUF k-quants) on CPU, GPU or NPU engines. NPU-aware engines have largely solved prefill. llm.npu (ASPLOS 2025) reports **more than 1,000 tok/s prefill for a billion-parameter model, 22.4× faster prefill and 30.7× energy savings on average** ([arXiv 2407.05858](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.05858)). Decode remains memory-bandwidth bound. PowerInfer-2 runs a 47B MoE on a phone at **11.68 tok/s with 19 GB of free RAM but only 2.13 tok/s with 7 GB** ([arXiv 2406.06282](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06282v3)). For low-resource devices the takeaway is that **available DRAM, not compute, sets usable model size and speed**. Engine comparisons remain confounded: MNN-LLM's speedups over llama.cpp and MLC-LLM come with mismatched quantization schemes, symmetric for MLC and asymmetric for the others ([arXiv 2506.10443](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10443v1)).

Native low-bit training is the most significant 2025 change. **BitNet b1.58 2B4T** is a natively trained ternary-weight 2B model trained on 4T tokens, with open weights and CPU/GPU kernels. A third-party comparison table, **not verified against the technical report**, lists **0.4 GB non-embedding memory, 29 ms/token CPU decode and about 0.028 J per inference (estimated)**. The same table gives LLaMA-3.2-1B 2 GB, 48 ms and 0.258 J, and Qwen2.5-1.5B 2.6 GB, 65 ms and 0.347 J. BitNet's average benchmark score is about 54.2 against 55.2 for Qwen2.5-1.5B ([HF Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/knoxel/bitnet-cpp-explorer/blob/main/app.py); [emelia.io](https://emelia.io/hub/bitnet-1bit-llm-cpu-inference)). bitnet.cpp kernels reportedly give **1.37–5.07× speedup with 55.4–70.0% energy reduction on ARM**, including Raspberry Pi, and **2.37–6.17× speedup with 71.9–82.2% energy reduction on x86** ([emelia.io](https://emelia.io/hub/bitnet-1bit-llm-cpu-inference)). Microsoft reportedly advises against commercial use without further testing. Apple's production on-device model, about 3B parameters, uses **2-bit quantization-aware training** plus KV-cache sharing. Secondary sources disagree on its effective bit-width, and one reports a 2/4-bit mix averaging 3.7 bits per weight ([arXiv 2507.13575](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.13575v2); [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2025/07/apple-foundation-models-ios26)). Once weights reach about 1.58 bits, **the bottleneck moves to activations and the KV cache**, which explains work such as TierKV ([arXiv 2609.21172](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.21172)) and FedQuad's activation quantization.

## Kilobyte communication is solved, while energy accounting barely exists

Bandwidth is the axis where the field is furthest ahead. Communication per round ranges from kilobytes to per-step activation streams across methods. At the low end, FedKSeed sends seeds and scalars (<18 KB) ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/qin24a.html)). Fed-SB trains 45–90× fewer parameters than LoRA ([arXiv 2502.15436](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.15436)). FedSA-LoRA uploads about half of LoRA, and logit distillation saves about 50% ([arXiv 2509.01750](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01750v2)). Split learning, which ships activations every step, sits at the high end. Federated pretraining borrows from low-communication distributed training. Photon (MLSys 2025) trains up to 7B with **64–512× less communication** and 35% lower wall time than its distributed baseline ([MLSys](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/185087ea328b4f03ea8fd0c8aa96f747-Abstract-Conference.html)). DiLoCo-family work shows that **2-bit quantized, top-k-sparsified pseudo-gradients** cost little quality: SparseLoCo reaches 97–99% sparsity, and MuLoCo matches uncompressed DiLoCo at 8× less communication ([arXiv 2508.15706](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15706v2); [papers.cool 2505.23725](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2505.23725)). These results come from datacenter or decentralized GPU settings with mostly IID shards. They are not from low-end devices.

Energy is the weakest axis. The best physical measurements in the notes are for classic CNN/RNN FL on a Raspberry Pi 4. INA219 sensors showed **communication raising power 8–32% above idle and training averaging about 3.3 W** ([ACM UCC 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3603166.3632545)). EcoFL (JLPEA 2026) estimates energy for emulated Pi 4 and Jetson Nano profiles with a **TDP-based model**, and its authors call hardware power-monitor validation an essential next step ([MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/jlpea16030024)). A 2025 survey notes that MLPerf Power covers Jetson boards but **has limited coverage of MCUs and ultra-low-power devices** ([PDF](https://anil.recoil.org/papers/2025-dl-rcn.pdf)). The only measured LLM energy figures in these notes are for inference (llm.npu, bitnet.cpp, the edge SLM study). The researchers drew a sharp consequence. ZO needs many forward passes, so **its communication savings could be partly or fully offset by on-device compute energy**. No paper found reports net joules per accuracy point for ZO-FL against LoRA-FL on the same hardware.

Benchmarks are starting to close this gap. FlowerTune (NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks) covers 4 domains and 26 base models and includes communication and memory metrics ([arXiv 2506.02961](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02961)). EdgeFlowerTune (2026) evaluates FedAvg+LoRA, FedProx+LoRA, HeteroLoRA and SplitLoRA on **Gemma-3-270M and Qwen2.5-0.5B under realistic edge constraints** ([arXiv 2605.08636](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08636)). A testbed survey catalogues physical FL testbeds with heterogeneous edge boards ([arXiv 2407.14154](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.14154)).

## A device-tier map of what works today

Taken together, the evidence supports a tier-based design rule. The rule is the synthesis of this report, not a claim made by any single paper. Clients with **8 GB or more of usable RAM** (flagship phones, Jetson Orin, edge servers) can run first-order 4-bit QLoRA on a 3–8B backbone. Heterogeneous ranks then pay off, and product-space or SVD-based aggregation (FlexLoRA, FedPA-LoRA) is the safer choice than zero-padding. Clients with **4–8 GB** sit at the frontier. The options are ZO on a 4-bit backbone, side-tuning such as Fed MobiLLM if a helper server exists, or split FL if the uplink can carry activations. Real evidence here reaches only OPT-1.3B on Jetson Xavier boards. Clients with **under 4 GB** should hold a 0.1–1.5B SLM, ideally 4-bit or natively ternary. The server LLM then learns from them through mutual distillation (FedMKT, FedProxy), and seed-based or logit-based exchange keeps bandwidth in the KB range. **MCU-class devices** are inference-only today, with micro-LMs of 30M parameters or fewer handing off to the cloud. The notes contain no evidence of federated LM training on MCUs.

| Device tier | Feasible training approach (evidence) | Inference approach | Main unknown |
|---|---|---|---|
| ≥8 GB (flagship phone, Orin, edge server) | 4-bit QLoRA + heterogeneous rank (FlowerTune recipe; FlexLoRA) | 4-bit 3–8B; NPU prefill | Measured energy and thermal behaviour over many rounds |
| 4–8 GB (mid-tier phone, Xavier) | ZO on quantized backbone; side-tuning (Fed MobiLLM, ≤1.3B real); split FL | 4-bit 1–3B or ternary 2B | ZO time-to-accuracy and J/round on device |
| <4 GB (low-end phone, Pi) | SLM-only client + server mutual KD (FedMKT, FedProxy) | Ternary/4-bit ≤1.5B (BitNet 2B4T ~0.4 GB, 2°) | Tokenizer heterogeneity; public-data dependence |
| MCU / Cortex-M | None found | BERT-scale (MCUBERT) or 8–30M micro-LM + cloud | Any ≥100M autoregressive generation |

## Research gaps: the evidence runs out at energy, mixed precision and real hardware

The open problems follow directly from where the measurements stop. The first is the **accuracy and time-to-accuracy gap between ZO and first-order tuning on physical hardware**. FedSPZO concedes the accuracy gap ([arXiv 2502.10239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10239)), CooperLLM's cloud correction implicitly concedes it ([arXiv 2601.12917](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.12917)), and the notes found no wall-clock ZO-FL run with measured joules on a low-end phone or Pi. The second is **aggregation when backbones are quantized differently per client** (4, 2 or 1.58 bits) and ranks also differ. HAFLQ and FedPipe vary quantization per client, but the notes found no FL paper that trains adapters against 2-bit or ternary backbones. The adapter-merge mathematics for differently quantized bases therefore remain unexplored. The third is **activations and KV cache as the binding memory**. FedQuad is the only federated work in the notes that quantizes activations ([arXiv 2506.01001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01001)), even though native low-bit weights make activations the dominant term.

The remaining gaps concern evaluation and deployment. **Energy accounting** relies on TDP emulation ([EcoFL](https://doi.org/10.3390/jlpea16030024)), and the only measured device-power numbers are for non-LLM FL ([ACM UCC 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3603166.3632545)). **Real-device scale** stops at about 1.3B parameters (Fed MobiLLM on Jetson; EdgeFlowerTune at 270M–0.5B), and phones appear in only one 2026 split-FL study ([Just Talk Once](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.01457)). **Heterogeneous-rank methods have no neutral benchmark under measured memory** ([FSLoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19389v4); [LoRA-FAIR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.14961)). **SLM federations** depend on a shared public dataset and on ad hoc token alignment ([FLoKD](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.13580)). **Intermittent connectivity** went unsearched once the search budget ran out. The DiLoCo-style asynchronous methods that tolerate weak links are validated on GPU clusters with IID shards ([arXiv 2508.15706](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15706v2)). **Privacy** remains open for split-FL activations and for DP combined with ZO, although DP-FedLoRA adds Gaussian noise to on-device LoRA ([arXiv 2509.09097](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.09097)). Finally, **production systems do no on-device LLM gradient training**. Google federates small LMs and privacy-preserving signals, and Apple uses on-device inference with a cloud fallback ([Google Research blog](https://www.research.google/blog/synthetic-and-federated-privacy-preserving-domain-adaptation-with-llms-for-mobile-applications/); [Apple AFM 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.13575v2)).

The researchers also flagged one combination that nobody has tried end to end. It pairs a **natively ternary SLM client with ZO or side-tuned adapters, seed-based communication à la FedKSeed, and SLM↔LLM distillation at the server**, measured on physical low-end hardware. Every component exists separately in the papers above, but no paper found combines them.

## Conclusion

The literature has largely solved the problem it posed in 2024, which was getting federated LLM tuning under a communication budget of kilobytes and a memory budget near inference. In doing so it has exposed a problem it has barely measured: compute energy and activation memory on real devices. The sharpest implication is methodological. Most "low-resource" results are GPU simulations with memory bookkeeping, so the rankings between ZO, side-tuning, split and distillation approaches could change once joules, thermal throttling and peak RSS on a Pi or a mid-tier phone are counted. The most valuable next contribution is probably not another aggregation rule. It is a physical-device benchmark that reports energy per accuracy point.

The second implication concerns where the field is converging. Heterogeneous-rank LoRA is mainly a solution for the top device tier, and the weakest clients are moving to an SLM-plus-distillation design. Native ternary models make that design more attractive each year. The federated LLM of 2027 may therefore be a federation of different small models that teach a shared large one, rather than one model with heterogeneous adapters. The aggregation question then becomes how to transfer knowledge across tokenizers and precisions, and today only token alignment and public data address it. These conclusions inherit the limits of the evidence: snippet-level reads, self-reported numbers and unverified [PK] identifiers. Verify the key figures against the primary PDFs before building on them.
