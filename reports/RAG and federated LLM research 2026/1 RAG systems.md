# Trained search agents overtake fixed RAG pipelines

Between 2024 and 2026, Retrieval-Augmented Generation stopped being one design (embed, retrieve top-k, read) and split into a few competing research programs. The most active is **reinforcement-learned search**. Starting with Search-R1 (COLM 2025), R1-Searcher, ZeroSearch and related work, the LLM learns *when* and *how* to query a retriever, using only an outcome reward ([Search-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09516v5); [ZeroSearch](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.04588)). The second is **structured, memory-like retrieval**, led by HippoRAG 2 (ICML 2025). Independent benchmarks show graph RAG helps on multi-hop and global questions but often ties or loses to naive or hybrid RAG on simple lookups, and some graph systems cost two to three orders of magnitude more prompt tokens ([HippoRAG 2](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/gutierrez25a.html); [GraphRAG-Bench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.05690)). The third is **vision-native document retrieval** (ColPali, ICLR 2025), which skips OCR entirely ([ColPali](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01449v1)). Underneath all three sit two sobering results. Retrievers still cannot reason: the best MTEB embedder scores **18.0 nDCG@10** on BRIGHT ([BRIGHT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.12883)). And long context has not replaced RAG; under LaRA (ICML 2025) the right choice depends on the model, task and context length, which turns "LC vs. RAG" into a per-query routing problem ([LaRA](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/li25dv.html)). Security and privacy became a sub-field of their own. A stealthy membership-inference attack needs only **~30 natural-language queries** to tell whether a document sits in a RAG index ([Riddle Me This, CCS 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.00306)), and federated RAG has only **18 primary studies** through 2025 ([FedRAG mapping study, EMNLP Findings 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18906)). The richest open problems sit where these lines meet: retrievers co-trained with RL reasoners, process-level rewards and evaluation for search agents, cost-aware routing among no-RAG, vanilla, graph and long-context modes, and private or federated retrieval tested against the newer stealthy attacks.

## The 2025 survey wave maps a field that has outgrown retrieve-then-read

The 2025 survey wave is useful mainly for its taxonomies and for showing where the field's own consensus lies. The largest systematic review screens **128 highly cited articles** from 2020 to May 2025 and gives 2025 papers a lower citation threshold. Its data therefore stops before most of the RL-search and 2026 graph work ([Brown et al., arXiv 2508.06401](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06401v3)). Sharma's survey sorts systems into **retriever-centric, generator-centric, hybrid and robustness-oriented** designs. It names adaptive retrieval, real-time retrieval integration, multi-hop reasoning and privacy-preserving retrieval as open problems, framed around two tensions: precision vs. flexibility and efficiency vs. faithfulness ([arXiv 2506.00054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00054)). The Agentic RAG survey, now at v4 (Apr 2026), organizes systems by agent cardinality, control structure and autonomy. Its sharpest claim is that **output-only metrics are insufficient**: benchmarks should score reasoning trajectories and cost ([arXiv 2501.09136v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.09136v4)). Domain surveys now exist as well, for example repository-level code RAG with hierarchical retrieval and memory compression ([arXiv 2510.04905](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04905v3)).

## Reinforcement learning turned retrieval into a learned search policy

The defining 2025 development is the "R1-style" wave. Instead of a fixed retrieve-then-read pipeline or prompted iterative retrieval, the generator is trained with RL to interleave reasoning tokens with search calls. **Search-R1** (UIUC-led, COLM 2025) set the template, which has three parts. Multi-turn search calls are embedded in RL rollouts. **Retrieved tokens are masked** out of the policy-gradient loss so the model is not trained to reproduce retrieved text, which keeps RL stable. The reward is simply whether the final answer is correct ([arXiv 2503.09516v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09516v5)). Its headline gains over RAG baselines across seven QA datasets are **[conflict]**: v3 reports **+41% (Qwen2.5-7B) and +20% (3B)** ([v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.09516v3)), while a later revision appears to report roughly 26% and 21% per search summaries. Researchers should cite the final version's numbers after checking v5. **R1-Searcher** (RUC) uses a two-stage outcome-based RL scheme so the model learns when and how to call retrieval. It reports beating prior RAG methods and even GPT-4o-mini on four multi-hop QA datasets ([arXiv 2503.05592](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.05592)).

The follow-on work reveals the area's real bottleneck: the **training environment**. Live web search during RL is costly and noisy, because API calls cost money and document quality is uncontrolled. **ZeroSearch** replaces the search engine with a simulated LLM "search engine" (3B parameters or larger) and uses a **curriculum that gradually degrades the quality of generated documents**, which removes API cost entirely ([arXiv 2505.04588](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.04588)). ZeroSearch's related-work section also gives the cleanest taxonomy of the area. One group trains against a local corpus (Search-R1, R1-Searcher). Another trains against live commercial search (DeepResearcher, WebThinker). ZeroSearch adds a third option, simulated search. **ReSearch** treats search operations as part of the reasoning chain, with no supervision on intermediate steps. Its arXiv ID (2503.25094) comes from a topic-aggregator snippet and is **unverified** ([Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/research)).

Agentic RAG more broadly, meaning multi-agent, graph-controlled and iterative retrieval without RL training, is mostly mapped by the survey above. Concrete 2026 systems in the notes are few. One is *Chain of Evidence*, which combines iterative RAG with pixel-level visual attribution ([arXiv 2605.01284](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01284)).

The Search-R1 family shares a lot: outcome-only rewards, small Qwen2.5 backbones, and the same multi-hop QA suites (NQ, HotpotQA, 2Wiki, MuSiQue). That makes results roughly comparable to each other, but also narrow. None of these papers, as captured in the notes, trains the **retriever**. The policy learns to work around a fixed retriever, which leaves BRIGHT-style retrieval failures (next section) untouched. The differences that matter for a researcher choosing a baseline are environment cost (local corpus < simulated < live web), realism (the reverse order), and reproducibility. Search-R1's open veRL-based codebase has become the reference implementation ([SourcePulse listing](https://www.sourcepulse.org/projects/1842941)).

## Graph retrieval wins on multi-hop questions but can cost 377 times more tokens

Graph-based RAG is the most benchmarked and most disputed sub-area. **HippoRAG 2** (OSU NLP, ICML 2025) runs Personalized PageRank over an LLM-built knowledge graph and adds deeper passage integration and online LLM filtering. It presents RAG as **non-parametric continual learning**: new knowledge is added to the index rather than the weights. It reports outperforming standard RAG on factual, sense-making and associative-memory tasks, with a **+7% associative-task gain over a state-of-the-art embedding model** ([arXiv 2502.14802](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.14802); [PMLR v267](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/gutierrez25a.html)). A third-party summary reports "+7.7", apparently against a different baseline **[2nd]** ([lunadong summary](https://papers.lunadong.com/paper/4542)).

The important 2025–2026 work is comparative, not new architectures. **GraphRAG-Bench** (HK PolyU, "When to use Graphs in RAG") builds a leaderboard on a novel corpus. HippoRAG 2 ranks first (56.48), with LightRAG and Microsoft GraphRAG lower ([arXiv 2506.05690](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.05690); [alphaXiv leaderboard](https://www.alphaxiv.org/benchmarks/the-hong-kong-polytechnic-university/graphrag-bench)). Its cost numbers are the most consequential finding in this section, though they come from a secondary summary **[2nd]**. Average prompt tokens per query are **879 for vanilla RAG, 1,008 for HippoRAG 2, 38,707 for MS-GraphRAG local search, 100,832 for LightRAG, and 331,375 for a global-search mode** (by context, MS-GraphRAG global) ([Substack summary](https://aiexpjourney.substack.com/p/do-you-really-need-graphrag-ai-innovations)). So the most expensive configuration uses about **377×** the tokens of vanilla RAG, while HippoRAG 2 leads on accuracy at roughly 1.15× vanilla cost.

Other benchmarks point the same way. On **WildGraphBench**, average accuracy is HippoRAG 2 64.33%, naive RAG 59.79%, and **LightRAG 56.76%, below naive RAG**. MS-GraphRAG global is best only on multi-fact questions (47.64%) **[2nd]** ([benchmarklist.com](https://benchmarklist.com/benchmarks/wildgraphbench/)). A legal-QA study (RAGE-KG 2025 workshop, CEUR-WS) compares HippoRAG 2, nano-GraphRAG, LightRAG and LlamaIndex against naive RAG. It finds **hybrid modes (LightRAG "mix", LlamaIndex hybrid) best and KG-only systems worse** ([CEUR-WS Vol-4079](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4079/paper6.pdf)). **RAGU** (2026) questions the evaluation methodology itself. It shows that **verbose outputs inflate GraphRAG scores** under LLM judging. With formatting controlled, its compact domain-adapted engine ties or slightly beats HippoRAG 2 on BioASQ (72.9 vs. 72.4), and HippoRAG 2 keeps a clear lead only on MuSiQue ([arXiv 2607.11683](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.11683)).

Four independent sources (GraphRAG-Bench, WildGraphBench, legal QA, RAGU) agree on one conclusion: **graph structure pays off for multi-hop and global "sense-making" questions, not for fact lookup**, and the expensive community-summary approaches are hard to justify unless the query really needs global synthesis. HippoRAG 2 is the exception that shows graph retrieval does not have to be expensive. Its PPR-over-passages design keeps prompts near vanilla size. The case for the opposing view is that global-mode GraphRAG was built for query-focused *summarization* over a whole corpus, a task that QA-style benchmarks under-sample. Its high token cost may be the right price for that task, and the benchmarks above say little about it. The original Microsoft GraphRAG paper and LightRAG paper were not verified in this session (see the unverified-pointers table).

## Retrievers still cannot reason, and documents are becoming images

Better generation cannot fix bad retrieval. **BRIGHT** (Princeton et al., ICLR 2025) shows how far retrieval lags on reasoning. Its queries in economics, psychology, mathematics and coding need reasoning to identify relevant documents, not lexical or semantic matching. The best MTEB embedding model reaches only **18.0 nDCG@10**. Having an LLM add reasoning to the query improves retrieval by up to **12.2 points**, and better retrieval lifts downstream QA by **6.6+ points** ([arXiv 2407.12883](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.12883); [ICLR 2025 proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/7a0f8055c838df8e62329a76c7c6403d-Abstract-Conference.html)). The query count (1,398 vs. 1,384) and the best score after query reasoning (24.3 vs. 22.1) differ across sources **[conflict]**. In both versions the state of the art stays below about 25 nDCG@10. The +12.2 gain from LLM query reasoning is the key diagnostic: the reasoning ability exists in the LLM but not in the embedder.

On the retrieval architecture side, **late interaction** has become the shared mechanism behind both text and visual retrieval. ColBERT encodes query and document separately, then scores them with a token-level MaxSim. Document vectors are precomputed offline, so query-time cost stays low ([Jina AI explainer](https://jina.ai/news/what-is-colbert-and-late-interaction-and-why-they-matter-in-search)). **Jina-ColBERT-v2** (31 Aug 2024) extends this to **89 languages** with Matryoshka-style flexible output dimensions and an **8,192-token** context, per the vendor's own description ([Jina AI](https://jina.ai/news/what-is-colbert-and-late-interaction-and-why-they-matter-in-search)).

**ColPali** (Illuin Tech / CentraleSupélec et al., ICLR 2025) applies late interaction to page images. It builds PaliGemma-based multi-vector patch embeddings and scores them with MaxSim, with **no OCR or layout pipeline**. It argues the real bottleneck in document RAG is ingestion, not the embedder, and it "largely outperforms" modern document-retrieval pipelines while being much faster and end-to-end trainable. It also introduced the **ViDoRe** benchmark ([arXiv 2407.01449](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01449v1); [ICLR 2025 poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/poster/28336)). **ColQwen2** swaps in a Qwen2-VL backbone for better retrieval and is commonly deployed on Vespa **[2nd]** ([Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2024/10/multimodal-retrieval-with-colqwen-vespa/)). **VisRAG** is described by a secondary source as pooling each page into a single VLM vector. That gives faster indexing and smaller storage at the cost of weaker recall. **The primary paper was not verified** ([traeai learning page](https://learn.traeai.com/t/ai-engineering/phases/12-multimodal-ai/23-colpali-vision-native-rag.html)).

## Long context did not replace RAG; it made retrieval a routing decision

The "is RAG dead?" debate driven by million-token context windows has a careful benchmark answer. **LaRA** (ICML 2025) builds **2,326 test cases** across four QA task types over novels, academic papers and financial statements, and evaluates **7 open and 4 proprietary LLMs**. It finds **no silver bullet**. The best choice depends on model size, long-context ability, context length, task type and chunk characteristics, and **long context does better on reasoning and comparison tasks** ([PMLR v267](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/li25dv.html); [arXiv 2502.09977](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.09977v2)). A third-party summary reports RAG beating LC by **38.12% on Mistral-Nemo-12B at 128k** while LC wins on GPT-4o **[2nd, not verified against the paper]** ([lunadong summary](https://papers.lunadong.com/paper/4999)). If that holds, it fits a simple hypothesis: RAG matters most for models whose effective context use lags their nominal window. As frontier models close that gap, the RAG advantage shrinks to cost and latency, but it does not disappear for weaker or cheaper models.

## Evaluation lags the systems it is meant to measure

Evaluation tools have improved, but their improvements depend on LLM judges whose biases now show up in published comparisons. **RAGChecker** (Amazon, NeurIPS 2024 Datasets & Benchmarks) breaks RAG quality into **claim-level metrics**. On the retriever side it measures claim recall and context precision. On the generator side it measures faithfulness, noise sensitivity, hallucination and context utilization. It correlates with human judgment significantly better than other metrics, was applied to eight RAG systems, and positions RAGAS, TruLens and ARES as fine-grained on only some metrics ([NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/27245589131d17368cccdfa990cbf16e-Abstract.html); [arXiv 2408.08067](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.08067v2)). RAGChecker still relies on an LLM to extract and check claims. RAGU's finding that answer verbosity inflates LLM-judged GraphRAG scores shows that judge bias is not a theoretical worry. It changes leaderboard order ([arXiv 2607.11683](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.11683)).

That framework benchmark is the only quantitative comparison of engineering stacks in the notes, and it shows **framework choice barely moves answer quality** on a basic pipeline ([GitHub](https://github.com/yourtechmafia/rag-frameworks-benchmark)). The 2026 positioning, drawn from vendor and blog sources **[2nd]**, is fairly consistent. LlamaIndex is best for ingestion and indexing breadth. LangChain plus LangGraph is for durable, stateful agentic retrieval. Haystack is for explicit, testable pipelines with built-in evaluators. RAGFlow is a turnkey self-hosted engine with deep PDF parsing. DSPy is "not a RAG framework in the classic sense" but a compiler that optimizes prompts and retrieval modules against a metric ([Firecrawl](https://www.firecrawl.dev/blog/best-open-source-rag-frameworks); [ScrapingBee](https://www.scrapingbee.com/blog/best-rag-frameworks/)). Blog-reported 2026 versions (LangChain 1.4.3, LangGraph 1.2.13, Haystack 3.3.0, RAGFlow 1.0.0-rc1, DSPy 3.4.0) and licenses (MIT for LlamaIndex, LangChain, LangGraph, DSPy, LightRAG; Apache-2.0 for Haystack, RAGFlow, txtai, Kotaemon) were **not checked against release pages** ([techsy.io](https://techsy.io/en/blog/best-rag-framework-2026)). For research, pick the framework by the experimental variable. DSPy suits program or prompt optimization, LangGraph suits agent control flow, and the reference codebases above (Search-R1, HippoRAG, colpali, RAGChecker) suit the sub-area baselines.

## Thirty queries suffice to expose a retrieval corpus

The common privacy argument for RAG is that sensitive data stays in an index rather than being memorized in weights. 2025 attack work undermines it. The **Interrogation Attack** ("Riddle Me This!", CCS 2025) is a black-box membership-inference attack that asks natural-language questions only the target document can answer. It succeeds with about **30 queries**, stays stealthy, and roughly **doubles TPR at 1% FPR** over prior RAG MIAs, which it shows are easy to detect ([arXiv 2502.00306](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.00306)). IBM's earlier black/gray-box MIA uses crafted prompts and proposes a prompt-template defense ([arXiv 2405.20446](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20446v2)). Two 2026 surveys organize the threat landscape. One builds a pipeline-centric taxonomy of **knowledge corruption, retrieval-time access manipulation, context exploitation and exfiltration**, and argues for layered, boundary-aware defenses ([arXiv 2604.08304](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08304v1)). The other catalogs membership-inference, index-inference and poisoning attacks as open challenges ([arXiv 2606.25533](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25533)). Privacy-aware decoding targets leakage at generation time ([arXiv 2508.03098](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.03098)), and a healthcare-specific review covers RAG privacy in clinical settings ([arXiv 2511.11347](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.11347)).

**Federated RAG** is where this connects to federated LLM research. The EMNLP 2025 Findings mapping study narrows about 50 candidates (2020–2025) to **18 primary studies**. It catalogs confidentiality techniques (TEEs, homomorphic encryption, differential privacy) and names privacy-preserving retrieval, cross-client heterogeneity and evaluation as open problems. It calls for shared benchmarks and stronger threat models, and gives C-FedRAG (clinical QA with records kept in-hospital) as an example ([arXiv 2505.18906](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18906); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.388.pdf)). The same review cites **RAGRoute**, which cuts redundant cross-silo queries ([ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.388.pdf)). **FedE4RAG** trains retriever embeddings federatedly with adaptive mutual knowledge distillation and dynamic gradient compression, reporting up to **94.89% communication reduction** ([ResearchGate mirror of arXiv 2504.19101](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391246945_Privacy-Preserving_Federated_Embedding_Learning_for_Localized_Retrieval-Augmented_Generation)). The agentic-FL notes instead describe FedE4RAG as using homomorphic encryption for retriever privacy **[conflict]**; it may use both. **HyFedRAG** is an edge-cloud federated RAG for heterogeneous medical data, evaluated on PMC-Patients ([arXiv 2509.06444](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06444)). Federated RAG is also becoming the substrate for cross-silo *agents*. **EPEAgent** applies minimal-disclosure controls at the RAG and context-retrieval stage of a "federated multi-agent system" ([Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Privacy-Enhancing-Paradigms-within-Federated-Shi-Wan/fb4446f32cfda955229d14824ed82581f6288a5e)). **FICAL** federates agents by sharing natural-language "knowledge compendiums" that are stored in a vector DB and retrieved at query time ([arXiv 2412.08054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.08054)).

Two inferences follow. First, the ~30-query MIA means any federated or private RAG system that protects only data *at rest* or *in transit* (TEE indexes, encrypted embeddings) still leaks through its *answers*. Defenses have to act at the retrieval-to-generation boundary, which is what both 2026 surveys and privacy-aware decoding point toward. Second, agentic RAG enlarges the attack surface. An agent that issues dozens of queries per task is both a better interrogator and a bigger target for poisoning, and the boundary-aware defenses proposed so far are still conceptual.

Taken together, gaps 1, 2 and 4 form one agenda. The field has learned search *policies* but not search *economics*: no published system in the notes jointly decides whether to retrieve, which retrieval structure to use, and how much to spend, while being trained on a signal that rewards good evidence rather than lucky answers. Gaps 6 and 7 form a second agenda, which is the most direct bridge to federated-LLM research. Federated RAG inherits every centralized RAG attack, and the 2025 attack results are stronger than the threat models most FedRAG systems were designed against.

## Conclusion

The 2024–2026 literature points to a shift in where RAG's difficulty lies. In 2023 the hard part was getting relevant text into the prompt. By 2026 the hard parts are *deciding* (whether to retrieve, with what structure, at what cost), *verifying* (whether a search trajectory and its evidence were good, not just whether the answer matched), and *bounding exposure* (what an index reveals through its answers). The RL-search wave solved one piece of the deciding problem, how to query a fixed retriever, and in doing so made the retriever's lack of reasoning the binding constraint. The graph-RAG benchmarks showed that structure is a cost-accuracy dial rather than an upgrade. The privacy results showed that "retrieval instead of memorization" protects less than its proponents assumed.

For a researcher, the strongest positioning is at these joins, not inside any single sub-area. Examples include a router that treats long context, graph retrieval and RL search as priced actions; a reward that credits evidence rather than outcomes; or a federated RAG protocol evaluated against a 30-query interrogator rather than a 2023 threat model. One caution applies to everything above: the evidence comes from abstracts and secondary summaries, several headline numbers conflict across versions, and GraphRAG comparisons are known to be sensitive to judge formatting. Reproducing key numbers from the primary PDFs is a prerequisite, not an extra.
