# Low-rank adapters no longer buy federated privacy

The main 2024–2026 finding is that federated LLM fine-tuning is **not private by construction, and sharing only LoRA adapters does not change that**. Text gradient inversion has become **exact at full batch sizes**: DAGER (NeurIPS 2024) recovers batches of up to 128 sequences with ROUGE above 0.99 ([arXiv 2405.15586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15586)). The 2026 attack MineGrad lets a malicious server reconstruct fine-tuning data **analytically from LoRA gradients, even when the number of tokens exceeds the adapter rank** ([arXiv 2608.01521](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.01521)). Membership inference now targets PEFT updates directly (ProjRes, LoRA-Leak, and ICP-MIA at NDSS 2026). Other clients' data can be extracted verbatim from the released federated model ([arXiv 2506.06060](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.06060)). Split-learning pipelines leak both prompts and responses through activations. On the defense side, **user-level differential privacy (DP-FedAvg, DP-FTRL, and BLT matrix mechanisms) is the only protection with formal guarantees that runs at consumer scale**. Google has shipped more than 20 Gboard LMs at ρ-zCDP 0.2–2 ([arXiv 2305.18465](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.18465)), but these are small on-device LMs, not multi-billion-parameter LLMs. Academic work in 2025–2026 concentrates on one structural problem: **DP noise is amplified through the LoRA product B·A** (FFA-LoRA, FedASK at NeurIPS 2025, LA-LoRA). Industry is instead moving server-side into **TEEs with verifiable DP** ([Google](https://research.google/pubs/confidential-federated-computations/)), and Google's own 2026 paper concedes that some side channels in that design cannot be removed ([arXiv 2603.21469](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.21469)). Secure aggregation and homomorphic encryption hide individual updates but defend neither against a model-manipulating server nor against leakage from the released model. Federated RAG and federated unlearning are still mostly preprints without benchmarks or certified guarantees. A caveat applies to everything below: **almost every 2025–2026 claim rests on search snippets and abstracts, not full-text reads**, and most 2026 items are non-peer-reviewed arXiv preprints. The provenance tags that follow mark this item by item.

## Text gradient inversion became exact, and LoRA does not stop it

Gradient inversion against language models has changed in kind since 2024, not just in degree. The pre-2024 line produced approximate reconstructions from small batches: TAG, LAMP ([arXiv 2202.08827](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.08827)) and FILM ([arXiv 2205.08514](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08514)) **[PK]**. **DAGER** replaced optimization with a membership test. Self-attention gradients are low-rank, and token embeddings are discrete, so DAGER checks whether each candidate token sequence lies in the span of the observed gradient. It uses exhaustive search for encoders and greedy search for decoders. The threat model is an honest-but-curious server with no data prior. DAGER reports **exact full-batch recovery up to batch size 128**, about **20× faster** than prior attacks at the same batch size, and on about **10× larger batches** ([arXiv 2405.15586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15586); [NeurIPS mirror](https://mlanthology.org/neurips/2024/petrov2024neurips-dager/)). The limits are documented too. **GPT-2 fails partly or completely at B = 64 and 128**, and decoder recovery degrades when batch × sequence length approaches the embedding dimension *d*. The structural defense this implies is aggregation over enough tokens to fill the gradient subspace **[Inf]**. In practice that rarely holds in cross-silo fine-tuning with small local batches.

The 2026 successors push in two directions. FedSpy-LLM ([arXiv 2604.06297](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.06297)) and TIGER ([arXiv 2606.18312](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.18312)) scale and generalize the subspace idea across architectures; for both papers **only the titles were visible [T]**. **MineGrad** matters more for practitioners. A malicious server shapes the setup so that fine-tuning data is embedded in the shared **LoRA gradients and can be read out analytically**. It works for language and vision, needs no adversarial pretraining on public data, and **works even when the number of training tokens exceeds the LoRA rank**, an assumption earlier PEFT attacks needed ([arXiv 2608.01521](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.01521)) **[S][PP]**. Geminio adds semantic targeting: the attacker describes the data it wants in natural language, and a vision-language model steers the malicious global model so that gradients leak the matching samples first ([arXiv 2411.14937](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.14937)). Its ICCV 2025 venue is **[U]**. These attacks continue the malicious-server line of Decepticons ([arXiv 2201.12675](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12675)) and "When the Curious Abandon Honesty" ([arXiv 2112.02918](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02918)) **[PK]**. Together they show that the server's control over the model is itself the attack surface.

The strongest counterargument comes from vision. "Practical Feasibility of Gradient Inversion Attacks in FL" concludes that **modern, performance-optimized image architectures largely resist meaningful reconstruction under realistic training settings**, and that many published successes depend on unrealistic choices ([arXiv 2508.19819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19819)). The study does not cover text. The two results are compatible once modality is taken into account **[Inf]**. In vision, inversion is a continuous optimization over pixels. In text, discreteness and low-rank attention gradients turn reconstruction into a verification problem, which makes text *easier* to invert exactly. A survey that pools "gradient inversion" across modalities would therefore misstate the risk for LLMs.

## Membership inference and extraction moved onto PEFT updates and the released model

Membership inference against federated LLMs split into two attack surfaces in 2025–2026: the per-client update and the final released model. On the update side, **ProjRes** starts from the observation that PEFT shrinks uploaded gradients and so narrows the classical gradient-based MIA surface. It proposes a projection-residual statistic on client updates and evaluates it against **7 baseline MIAs, 4 datasets and 4 LLMs** ([arXiv 2604.21197](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21197v1)) **[S][PP]**. LoRA-Leak builds a MIA framework specifically for LoRA-fine-tuned models ([arXiv 2507.18302](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.18302)). On the model side, **ICP-MIA** (NDSS 2026) probes membership through in-context behaviour rather than shadow fine-tuning. It reports that **higher LoRA rank increases MIA success, while 4-bit QLoRA appears to reduce it** ([NDSS PDF](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2026-f892-paper.pdf)). This bears directly on federated practice, because the FlowerTune reference recipe of **4-bit quantization + LoRA rank 8 + FedProx** ([Flower app](https://flower.ai/apps/mrs83/flowertune_qwen2.5-7b-instruct-medical)) sits in the low-risk corner of ICP-MIA's grid without anyone having designed it for privacy **[Inf]**. The first PEFT-specific defense to appear is FISGuard, which constrains federated adapter updates to fixed input subspaces ([arXiv 2608.27836](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.27836)) **[S][PP]**. Its utility cost and its robustness against adaptive attacks were not visible.

Extraction results fit the theory of user-level clustering. Earlier Google work found that FedAvg alone reduces unintended memorization and that DP-FedAvg reduces it further ([Thakkar et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342198125_Understanding_Unintended_Memorization_in_Federated_Learning); [Ramaswamy et al., arXiv 2009.10031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.10031)). A survey reports that FL plus DP can drive canary extraction **to zero in some cases** ([arXiv 2312.06717](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.06717)). The 2025 result that changes the picture is "Simple Yet Effective": it shows **verbatim extraction of other clients' private data** from a federated fine-tuned LLM. Federated models memorize **less than centralized ones, but still leak** ([arXiv 2506.06060](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.06060)). Two 2026 papers connect privacy to integrity. "Loss Landscape Poisoning" shows that poisoned data can shape a model so that a *later-seen* target's data becomes extractable ([arXiv 2606.17110](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.17110)) **[PP]**. That threat model fits cross-silo federated fine-tuning with one adversarial participant. On the mitigation side, EP-MPD performs **cross-client deduplication with private set intersection-style cryptography** before federated LM training, which removes the duplicates that drive memorization ([arXiv 2407.08152](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.08152); [ePrint 2024/1151](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/1151.pdf)).

**Attribute and property inference remains a blind spot.** The canonical result, Melis et al. (S&P 2019, [arXiv 1805.04049](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.04049)) **[PK]**, has no 2025–2026 counterpart specific to federated LLMs within the search budget. The surveys that cover it ([The Federation Strikes Back, ACM CSUR](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3724113); [arXiv 2508.13730](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.13730), more than 200 papers) do so across model families. The adjacent poisoning literature shows a structural tension. Secure aggregation and HE hide individual updates and therefore **disable server-side anomaly detection and robust aggregation**. Pasquini et al. showed that a malicious server can evade SecAgg by sending different models to different clients ([arXiv 2110.07714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.07714)) **[PK]**. Harmful fine-tuning, which removes safety alignment through fine-tuning data, is surveyed in [arXiv 2409.18169](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.18169).

## Split learning trades client compute for activation leakage

Split federated learning has become the default answer to the memory wall on edge devices. Recent systems include FedsLLM ([arXiv 2504.14667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14667v2)), memory-efficient SFL for heterogeneous phones ([arXiv 2506.02940](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02940)) and "Just Talk Once", tested on real smartphones ([arXiv 2609.01457](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.01457)). **None of these systems papers reported formal privacy guarantees in the snippets seen** (cross-reference from the edge-FL notes). The privacy literature shows that this omission matters. The 2026 survey of split learning for LLM fine-tuning documents input reconstruction from "smashed data" (the transmitted activations). It describes **BiSR**, a bidirectional attack that combines forward-activation inversion with backward gradient matching ([arXiv 2604.24468](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.24468v1)). **PrivPair** shows a "zero-cost decoder": the server simply runs the client's split layers and output head on the activations it receives, and **reconstruction quality improves as fine-tuning proceeds** ([arXiv 2609.09794](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.09794v1)) **[S][PP]**. **PIDI** attacks a head-body-tail split LLM at inference time and recovers **both prompts and generated responses** ([arXiv 2606.14210](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.14210)) **[S][PP]**. SIRA shows passive semantic text reconstruction against split-learning LM phishing detectors ([Springer IJIS 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10207-026-01311-4)).

The defenses lag the attacks. PrivPair and PIDI each propose a mitigation, with PrivPair evaluating DP on smashed data. Neither paper's privacy-utility numbers were visible **[S]**. The same tension recurs here as with LoRA **[Inf]**: the head-body-tail split moves the output head to the client for privacy, but the zero-cost-decoder result implies that a server holding the body can approximate the client layers anyway as fine-tuning aligns them. Black-box prompt tuning is an orthogonal design. FDPT (ICCV 2025) uses zeroth-order discrete prompt tuning partly to reduce inversion risk ([CVF PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2025/papers/Wu_FDPT_Federated_Discrete_Prompt_Tuning_for_Black-Box_Visual-Language_Models_ICCV_2025_paper.pdf)), and a 2025 model-accessibility survey rates federated black-box prompt tuning through APIs as the most privacy-preserving option ([arXiv 2508.16261](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.16261)). Neither claim has been tested against a dedicated text-prompt inversion attack.

## User-level DP is the only defense deployed at scale, but LoRA amplifies its noise

Production evidence comes almost entirely from Google. Gboard is the reference deployment: **more than 20 LMs trained and deployed with ρ-zCDP between 0.2 and 2** using DP-FTRL, two of them also with secure aggregation. **Every new Gboard LM FL task now requires DP**, and the first formally-DP production model (Spanish next-word prediction) launched in **February 2022** ([arXiv 2305.18465](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.18465); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-industry.60.pdf)). Google's research blog calls the **ε≈10** guarantees of many launched LMs "reasonable for ML in practice" ([Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/advances-in-private-training-for-production-on-device-language-models/)); the blog's date is **[U]**. On the mechanism side, **BLT (Buffered Linear Toeplitz) matrix factorization** replaces tree-aggregation DP-FTRL as a practical production mechanism ([arXiv 2408.08868](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.08868)). Separately, architectures such as SI-CIFG were tuned for DP in live experiments on millions of keyboard devices ([arXiv 2403.08100](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.08100)). The algorithmic roots are DP-FedAvg ([arXiv 1710.06963](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06963)) and DP-FTRL ([arXiv 2103.00039](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00039)) **[PK]**.

How Google applies this to LLMs is telling. Its 2025 approach does **not** train an LLM on devices with DP. Instead, DP-FL collects privacy-preserving signals that steer LLM-generated synthetic data, and that data fine-tunes the server-side Gboard Proofread LLM ([Google Research blog, July 2025](https://www.research.google/blog/synthetic-and-federated-privacy-preserving-domain-adaptation-with-llms-for-mobile-applications/), snippet only **[S]**). An earlier result showed that pretraining on synthetic data generated from public LLM prompts improved next-word-prediction accuracy by **19.0% and 22.8% relative** to a model trained on a standard public dataset ([arXiv 2404.04360](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.04360)). The research notes found **no public evidence of production user-level DP federated fine-tuning of a multi-billion-parameter LLM**. The deployment frontier is therefore "DP-FL on small models + synthetic data for big models", not "DP-FL on big models" **[Inf]**.

The academic DP-for-LLM front is about one algebraic problem. FFA-LoRA (ICLR 2024, [arXiv 2403.12313](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12313) **[PK]**) observed that noise added to both LoRA factors is **amplified through the product B·A**, and proposed freezing A and training only B. **FedASK** (NeurIPS 2025) names the resulting dilemma: noising both adapters amplifies noise, while freezing one limits learnability. It resolves the dilemma with a **two-stage sketching pipeline** that updates both A and B under DP ([arXiv 2507.09990](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09990)). An independent review on Pith **questions whether its two central theorems hold** ([Pith](https://pith.science/paper/2507.09990)) **[U]**. **LA-LoRA** formalizes the LoRA update as *semi-quadratic*: DP noise produces a non-Gaussian quadratic noise term that destabilizes training. Its remedy is **alternating local updates of A and B plus a low-pass smoothing filter** ([arXiv 2602.19926](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19926v1); [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=BPzSV4uw0x)) **[PP]**. Applied variants add Gaussian noise to LoRA matrices with budget-calibration guidance (DP-FedLoRA, [arXiv 2509.09097](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.09097)), allocate matrix-wise, sensitivity-aware noise across clients with heterogeneous ranks (iP-FedLoRA, [ACM](https://doi.org/10.1145/3770855.3817914)), or adapt DP by domain for mental-health LLMs (FedMentor, [arXiv 2509.14275](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14275)). **No accuracy-versus-ε or perplexity-versus-ε tables for Llama-class federated DP fine-tuning could be retrieved**. The comparative claim that "method X beats method Y at ε=…" therefore cannot be made from this evidence. Note also that most academic DP-LoRA work reports *record-level* or *client-level* guarantees on simulated silos. That is a different unit from Gboard's *user-level* guarantee over millions of devices **[Inf]**.

## Cryptography and TEEs hide updates but not the released model

Cryptographic aggregation for LLMs has converged on **CKKS homomorphic encryption combined with model sparsity**. The classical protocols are pairwise-masking SecAgg ([ePrint 2017/281](https://eprint.iacr.org/2017/281)) and SecAgg+ with polylogarithmic overhead ([ePrint 2020/704](https://eprint.iacr.org/2020/704)) **[PK]**. **FedShield-LLM** combines LoRA, unstructured pruning and CKKS FHE for encrypted-domain aggregation. It claims to be the first to pair FHE with pruning in federated LLM fine-tuning, with mitigation of training and inference leakage ([arXiv 2506.05640](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.05640)) **[PP][S]**. **SecFFT** encrypts only the high-level, semantically rich parameters to cut HE cost ([Frontiers in AI 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2025.1690950/full)); a correction was published in December 2025 ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12750460/)). PrivTuner pairs HE with LoRA for PEFT of foundation models ([arXiv 2410.00433](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.00433)). On the protocol side, **multiparty threshold HE** lets a subset of online clients decrypt the aggregate, which handles dropout, and combining compression with encryption keeps overhead small ([arXiv 2503.00581](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00581)). The standard framing is that HE adds no noise and so loses no utility, at much higher compute than masking SecAgg. That framing comes from an aggregator page ([Emergentmind](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/federated-security-for-llms)) and should be treated as low-confidence. **No 2025–2026 MPC-specific federated-LLM system surfaced** beyond HE and threshold HE.

The decisive limitation is the threat model, not the cost **[Inf]**. Secure aggregation and HE protect individual updates from an honest-but-curious server. They do nothing against three of the threats above. A **malicious server** can manipulate the model (MineGrad, Geminio) or send inconsistent models (Pasquini et al.). The **released global model** can be queried for extraction (arXiv 2506.06060). And **activations** leak in split designs. DP on the released model is therefore still required, and cryptography complements it rather than replacing it.

Google's TEE turn is the most consequential systems change of the period. **Confidential Federated Computations** combines TEEs, open-sourced and reproducibly built server binaries, and a ledger that enforces device-authorized access policies. Devices pre-authorize which TEE workloads may touch their uploads, and only anonymized or DP outputs leave. The result is **externally verifiable** server-side privacy ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/confidential-federated-computations/); code at [google-parfait/confidential-federated-compute](https://github.com/google-parfait/confidential-federated-compute), verified). The design already runs in production for confidential federated analytics, namely Gboard new-word discovery ([blog](https://research.google/blog/discovering-new-words-with-confidential-federated-analytics/)). Google has announced a next-generation FL system that uses TEEs to deliver **verifiable DP** while moving computation server-side to cut training time ([blog](https://research.google/blog/toward-provably-private-learning-from-federated-data/); [X post](https://x.com/GoogleResearch/status/2106041109944357182)). **The date, ε values and model sizes of that system are unverified [U]**. Google's own 2026 hardening paper states that **some side channels are inevitable** in systems built from multiple confidential VMs, and that an insider could exploit them to **circumvent the DP guarantees**. It proposes mitigations ([arXiv 2603.21469](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.21469)). The trust anchor thus moves from the server operator to AMD and Intel TEE correctness ([Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/identity-security/verifiable-trust-in-the-ai-era-whats-new-in-confidential-computing)). The strategic implication **[Inf]** is that TEE-based FL reopens server-side training of large models, which on-device FL could not support, at the price of hardware trust.

## Federated RAG and unlearning remain preprint-stage, with few benchmarks

Federated RAG is the newest privacy surface. It sits between FL and retrieval: each silo keeps its corpus and possibly its own embedder, and queries or retrieved context cross the trust boundary. The **Federated RAG systematic mapping study** (Findings of EMNLP 2025) narrowed about 50 candidates from 2020–2025 to **only 18 primary studies**. It catalogues confidentiality techniques (TEE, HE, DP) and names privacy-preserving retrieval, cross-client heterogeneity and evaluation as open problems. C-FedRAG for clinical QA is an example ([arXiv 2505.18906](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18906); [ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.388.pdf)). FedE4RAG trains retriever embeddings federatively with mutual knowledge distillation and gradient compression, reporting up to **94.89% communication reduction** ([ResearchGate mirror of arXiv 2504.19101](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391246945_Privacy-Preserving_Federated_Embedding_Learning_for_Localized_Retrieval-Augmented_Generation)). The agentic-AI notes, citing the review, describe it as using **homomorphic encryption for retriever privacy**. The two characterizations differ, and FedE4RAG plausibly uses both **[U]**. HyFedRAG builds an edge-cloud federated RAG for heterogeneous medical data, evaluated on PMC-Patients ([arXiv 2509.06444](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06444)). PRAG claims end-to-end privacy-preserving RAG ([arXiv 2604.26525](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.26525)) **[T]**.

The attack literature from centralized RAG shows how weak "the data stays in the index" is as a privacy argument. The **Interrogation Attack** (CCS 2025) runs black-box membership inference using natural-language questions that only the target document can answer. It succeeds with **about 30 queries**, achieves roughly **2× TPR at 1% FPR** over prior attacks, and stays stealthy ([arXiv 2502.00306](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.00306)). Earlier RAG MIAs used crafted prompts and a prompt-template defense ([arXiv 2405.20446](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20446v2)). Privacy-aware decoding ([arXiv 2508.03098](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.03098)) and two 2026 surveys covering membership inference, index inference and poisoning ([arXiv 2606.25533](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25533); [arXiv 2604.08304](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08304v1)) round out the centralized picture. **No federated RAG system in the notes is evaluated against Interrogation-style MIAs**. That is the most concrete gap in this sub-area **[Inf]**. A related, explicitly non-FL strand is the "federated multi-agent system", where agents cooperate across silos through minimal-disclosure retrieval rather than shared gradients (EPEAgent, [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Privacy-Enhancing-Paradigms-within-Federated-Shi-Wan/fb4446f32cfda955229d14824ed82581f6288a5e)). The 2026 Federated Agent Optimization paper finds that **none of the text- or rule-exchanging agent-FL methods applies a formal privacy mechanism** ([arXiv 2610.01195](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.01195v1)).

Federated unlearning for LLMs is at the framework stage. **Oblivionis** supports federated LLM fine-tuning together with targeted unlearning, for example for right-to-be-forgotten requests ([arXiv 2508.08875](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.08875)) **[PP]**. Hierarchical federated unlearning for LLMs ([arXiv 2510.17895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.17895)) is **[T]**. The broader LLM unlearning surveys are not FL-specific ([arXiv 2510.25117](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25117), more than 180 papers; [Artif. Intell. Rev. 58:399](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-025-11376-7); [awesome-machine-unlearning](https://github.com/tamlhp/awesome-machine-unlearning)). None of the federated unlearning work found offers a certified guarantee. The only rigorous options remain retraining or DP-based deniability **[Inf]**. Federated unlearning has an additional complication: the server cannot inspect the forgotten client's data under SecAgg, so verifying that a client was forgotten is itself an open privacy problem.

## The defenses cover different threats, so none is sufficient alone

Setting the 2024–2026 attacks against the defenses yields a coverage matrix. The matrix is this report's synthesis **[Inf]** and is built from the threat models in the cited papers. Its main lesson is that every deployed defense leaves at least one modern attack uncovered. **DP on the released model is the only control that addresses extraction and membership inference on the final artifact**. **Cryptography and TEEs are the only controls that hide individual updates without a utility cost**. **Nothing in the evidence base fully addresses a malicious server that manipulates the model** except verifiable server code inside TEEs, and that rests on hardware trust.

| Defense | Honest-but-curious inversion (DAGER) | Malicious-server inversion (MineGrad, Geminio) | MIA / extraction on released model | Split-activation leakage (PrivPair, PIDI) | Utility cost | Compute cost | Trust anchor | Maturity for LLMs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| User-level DP (DP-FTRL, BLT) | Bounds leakage formally | Bounds leakage if clipping and noise are client-side or verifiable | Yes (formal) | Only if applied to activations | High for LoRA (noise amplification) | Low | Correct noise implementation | Production for small LMs only |
| DP-LoRA variants (FFA-LoRA, FedASK, LA-LoRA) | Yes (formal) | Same as above | Yes | No | Reduced vs naive DP-LoRA; no shared ε benchmark | Low | Same | Research |

## Tooling and regulation lag behind the threat model

Framework coverage is the weakest-evidenced part of this report, because the framework searches never ran. The only framework artifact verified in search is Google's **Parfait confidential-federated-compute** repository ([GitHub](https://github.com/google-parfait/confidential-federated-compute)). The cross-referenced FL-for-LLM notes verify the federated *fine-tuning* frameworks but not their privacy features. **FlowerTune** is the NeurIPS 2025 Datasets & Benchmarks leaderboard for federated LLM fine-tuning across four domains ([arXiv 2506.02961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02961)). **NVIDIA FLARE** supports PEFT and full SFT of foundation LLMs ([arXiv 2402.07792](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.07792)). Flower apps run natively on the FLARE runtime ([arXiv 2407.00031](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.00031)). FATE-LLM is an industrial PEFT framework ([arXiv 2310.10049](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.10049)). The privacy features listed in the table below come from background knowledge. The practical conclusion holds regardless of version details **[Inf]**: **no mainstream framework offers turnkey, audited user-level DP plus secure aggregation for multi-billion-parameter federated LoRA**. Composing Flower or NVFlare with Opacus-style clipping by hand will meet the LoRA noise-amplification problem that FedASK and LA-LoRA describe.

Regulation enters through two instruments. Both mappings below are **[PK]**, because the regulatory searches did not run. Under the **GDPR** ([Regulation 2016/679](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)), FL is generally read as supporting data minimisation (Art. 5(1)(c)) and data protection by design (Art. 25). But model updates and models can still be personal data unless they meet the Recital 26 anonymisation bar, and Art. 17 erasure is what motivates federated unlearning. Under the **EU AI Act** ([Regulation 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)), Art. 10 permits processing special-category data for bias detection in high-risk systems subject to "state-of-the-art security and privacy-preserving measures", and general-purpose AI model provider obligations (Art. 53) apply from August 2025. The Federation Strikes Back survey includes a policy-landscape section mapping FL privacy to regulation ([ACM CSUR](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3724113)). The technical evidence in this report bears on one open legal question **[Inf]**. Verbatim cross-client extraction from federated LLMs ([arXiv 2506.06060](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.06060)) and stealthy RAG membership inference ([arXiv 2502.00306](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.00306)) argue **against treating a federated LLM or a federated retrieval index as anonymous without DP**. And no federated unlearning method yet produces evidence that a regulator could audit. FL-specific guidance from EDPB, EDPS or ICO for 2025–2026 was **not retrieved** and needs to be checked separately.

## Conclusion

The evidence moves the central question of federated LLM privacy from "what do updates leak?" to "whom must the client trust, and what does the released model reveal?" Text inversion is exact, adapters leak beyond their rank, and the final model leaks across clients. **Privacy therefore has to be enforced as a formal property of the released artifact (DP) and as a verifiable property of the server (attested code), not as a side effect of FL's architecture or of LoRA's compression.** Industry and academia currently take complementary halves of that. Google bets on verifiable DP inside TEEs, and on synthetic data to reach large models without on-device LLM training. Academia is repairing the algebra of DP-LoRA. Neither has yet shown user-level DP federated fine-tuning of a multi-billion-parameter LLM with a published privacy-utility curve.

For a researcher entering the area, the highest-leverage contributions are those that join these strands. The first is a DP-friendly PEFT parameterization evaluated on a shared benchmark with standardized MIA and canary audits. The second is a cheap client-side check against model-manipulating servers that does not depend on TEEs. The third is a formal privacy notion for split-learning activations. The most pressing verification task is reading the 2026 preprints this report could only see through snippets. Several of its sharpest claims, MineGrad's beyond-rank result above all, rest on abstracts alone.
