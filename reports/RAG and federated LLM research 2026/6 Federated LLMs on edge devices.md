# Device memory, not compute, shapes federated edge LLMs

The 2024–2026 literature on federated LLMs at the edge is organized around one constraint: **device memory for backpropagation**, especially activation memory. Almost every successful design works by keeping activations, optimizer state or trainable parameters off the device. Forward-only and zeroth-order training (FwdLLM, FedKSeed, FedSPZO, CooperLLM) cuts device memory to inference level and communication to kilobytes, but needs many forward passes and still trails first-order LoRA in accuracy. Server-assisted side-tuning and split federated learning (Fed MobiLLM, FedsLLM, Just Talk Once) keep first-order accuracy, but they move the cost to the uplink and expose activations, which 2026 attacks can invert. Heterogeneous-rank LoRA (HetLoRA, FlexLoRA, FLoRA, FSLoRA, FedPA-LoRA) has become its own sub-field, but it only helps clients that can already hold the backbone. On the inference side, NPU engines have largely solved on-device prefill (llm.npu and HeteroLLM report more than **1,000 tok/s**), and the open levers are now memory-bound decode and device–edge–cloud speculative decoding. Collaborative speculative decoding gives real but modest gains (about **1.1× to 35%**). The evidence base is still thin in three ways. Real-device federated fine-tuning has been shown only up to about **1.3B parameters**. No study measures energy per round for federated LLM fine-tuning on a phone or Jetson board. And no production system yet runs LLM gradient updates on phones: Google uses DP-federated *signals* to steer server-side synthetic data, and Apple routes requests between a roughly 3B on-device model and Private Cloud Compute. For a researcher, the clearest openings are joules-per-accuracy benchmarking on physical devices, private split learning with formal guarantees, aggregation across clients with different quantization levels, and co-designing federated personalization with collaborative inference.

## Three families escape the on-device backprop memory wall

The notes converge on a diagnosis. The binding constraint for on-device federated fine-tuning is **memory, not FLOPs**, because storing activations for backpropagation dominates device memory. Fed MobiLLM's authors make this argument directly, as does FwdLLM, the forward-gradient system they position against ([Fed MobiLLM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06765); [FwdLLM](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc24/presentation/xu-mengwei)). Three families of methods respond to it, and a fourth, SLM↔LLM distillation, sidesteps the problem by never putting the large model on the device at all.

### Forward-only and zeroth-order federated tuning cut memory to inference level

FwdLLM (USENIX ATC 2024) set the template. It replaces backpropagation with "perturbed inferences" (forward gradients) on top of LoRA or adapters, so a gradient estimate costs about two inferences and stores no activations. It can run on mobile NPUs that only support inference, which it reports as **19.5–27.5× faster than computing a backward gradient on CPU** ([USENIX ATC'24](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc24/presentation/xu-mengwei)). The journal extension FwdLLM+ (IEEE TMC 2026) adds low-rank perturbations and claims **up to 151× faster training and 93% less memory** than backprop-based FedLLM ([IEEE CSDL](https://www.computer.org/csdl/journal/tm/2026/08/11429542/2eOD933hNSw)). FedKSeed attacks the communication side. Clients exchange only (seed, scalar-gradient) pairs drawn from a finite pool of seeds, so full-parameter tuning of billion-scale models costs **under 18 KB per round** ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/qin24a.html)). FedKSeed also claims that FwdLLM can need several hundred GB of communication for a model of about 300M parameters. That figure is a competitor's claim and has not been checked.

The 2025–2026 work in this family no longer asks whether zeroth-order federated learning converges. It asks how to make it efficient and accurate. FedSPZO uses different numbers of perturbations for different blocks, reports **up to 3× lower compute** than other zeroth-order FL methods up to LLaMA-3.2-3B, and concedes that it still **trails first-order LoRA** ([arXiv 2502.10239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10239)). Meerkat targets non-IID drift with transferable sparse updates ([arXiv 2506.03337](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03337v1)). CooperLLM (Jan 2026) is the most telling design. Devices run zeroth-order updates, and the cloud corrects them using gradients from its own backpropagation ([arXiv 2601.12917](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.12917)). In other words, pure on-device zeroth-order training is treated as too slow or too inaccurate to stand alone.

### Server-assisted side-tuning and split federated learning keep first-order accuracy

The second family keeps exact first-order gradients but moves the trainable state to an edge server. MobiLLM is a single-device design. The phone runs only forward passes through a frozen backbone, and a server-side **side network** does the backpropagation. It reports **4.487 GB** of device memory for OPT-1.3B ([arXiv 2502.20421](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20421)). Fed MobiLLM (Aug 2025) makes this federated. Devices upload selected intermediate activations, and the server trains and aggregates the side networks. Its testbed, however, is **Jetson TX2, Xavier NX and AGX Xavier, not phones**, with OPT models from 125M to 1.3B ([arXiv 2508.06765](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06765)). PAE MobiLLM adds a privacy-aware additive variant that keeps data and labels local ([arXiv 2507.01216](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01216)).

Split federated learning (SFL) cuts the model itself. In the HKUST split-federated-LoRA design (IEEE TWC 2025, with a GLOBECOM 2024 version), the heavy encoder sits on the edge server, and the embedding and task modules stay on the device ([arXiv 2407.02924](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.02924v1)). FedsLLM jointly optimizes the split point, LoRA rank, subchannel allocation and transmit power to minimize training delay. It reports comparable accuracy with much lower client compute ([arXiv 2504.14667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14667v2)). A memory-efficient SFL design for heterogeneous phones fine-tunes LoRA on the lower layers on the device, while the server handles the matching adapters one device at a time. It reports **79% memory reduction and 6% less training time**, but a third-party review says the 79% figure comes from **BERT-base** experiments ([arXiv 2506.02940](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02940); [review](https://www.themoonlight.io/tw/review/memory-efficient-split-federated-learning-for-llm-fine-tuning-on-heterogeneous-mobile-devices)). The 2026 paper *Just Talk Once* stands out for its hardware. It evaluates on a real wireless testbed in which **three of the five clients are Huawei nova 9 Pro smartphones**. That is one of the very few phone-based federated LLM fine-tuning testbeds in the notes ([arXiv 2609.01457](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.01457)).

The cost of this family is structural. Split learning sends activations up and gradients down **every step**, and the server processes clients in sequence. That is why the wireless papers co-design the split point and LoRA rank with radio resources.

### Heterogeneous-rank LoRA turned aggregation into its own sub-field

The third family assumes that every client can hold a (possibly quantized) backbone and adapts the *amount* of training to each device. The methods are best compared by how they aggregate LoRA factors of different ranks. HetLoRA (Google, EMNLP 2024) zero-pads factors to the maximum rank, truncates them afterward, and uses sparsity-weighted aggregation with self-pruning ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.717)). FLoRA (NeurIPS 2024) argues that naively averaging the A and B matrices is **mathematically flawed**, because the average of products is not the product of averages. It stacks client factors instead, which aggregates exactly ([Sony AI](https://ai.sony/publications/flora-federated-fine-tuning-large-language-models-with-heterogeneous-low-rank-adaptations)). The price is that the global rank grows with the number of clients ([FedPA-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.15381)). FlexLoRA (NeurIPS 2024) aggregates the full products ΔW and redistributes rank-specific factors by SVD. It beats HetLoRA on zero-shot generalization to unseen clients ([arXiv 2402.11505](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11505)). In FSLoRA's own experiments it also beats FLoRA (**77.90 vs 72.81** average under a normal rank distribution) ([FSLoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19389v4)). The 2025–2026 refinements are sketching (FSLoRA), product-space aggregation that **decouples compute rank from communication rank** (FedPA-LoRA, Aug 2026), and folding (Fed-PLoRA, ICLR 2026, known only from a secondary notes site) ([papernotes](https://en.papernotes.org/ICLR2026/llm_safety/heterogeneous_federated_fine-tuning_with_parallel_one-rank_adaptation/)). FedSA-LoRA (ICLR 2025) takes a different route. It shares only the A matrices, on the finding that A captures general knowledge and B captures client-specific knowledge ([ICLR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.01463)).

The more systems-oriented methods target edge servers rather than phones. FedPipe plans, for each heterogeneous private edge server (hospitals are the example), which weights to adapt, the LoRA rank, the batch size and how far to quantize the base model. It formulates this as an MILP solved with a heuristic and reports faster training and higher accuracy, though the notes give no numbers. It also tackles the straggler problem that uneven resources create ([arXiv 2404.06448](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.06448v2)). HAFLQ quantizes the frozen backbone block by block according to salience and reports **31% memory reduction, on text classification only** ([arXiv 2411.06581](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06581v2)). FedQuad adapts LoRA depth per device and **quantizes activations**, which targets the term that actually dominates memory on small devices ([arXiv 2506.01001](https://export.arxiv.org/abs/2506.01001)). FedRA (ECCV 2024) gives each client a random subset of layers each round, so training works even when **no client can hold the full model** ([arXiv 2311.11227](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11227)).

### Small models on the device, the large model on the server

For truly low-end clients with 4 GB of RAM or less, the notes' strongest inference is that rank heterogeneity does not help, because these clients cannot hold the backbone at all. The workable design is a **small model on the device, the LLM on the server, and mutual distillation between them**. In FedMKT (COLING 2025), client SLMs with different architectures and tokenizers exchange knowledge with a server LLM through minimum-edit-distance token alignment and selective mutual distillation. Both the LLM and the SLMs improve ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.17/)). FedProxy (Apr 2026) reports that offsite-tuning methods **consistently underperform centralized fine-tuning**, and it beats them with proxy SLMs and heterogeneity-aware fusion ([arXiv 2604.19015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.19015)). Logit-based federated distillation over wireless links cuts inter-client communication by about **50%** ([arXiv 2509.01750](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01750v2)). FLoKD (Sep 2026) criticizes FedMKT for using public data without judging its relevance ([arXiv 2609.13580](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.13580)).

Two points follow from this table. First, zeroth-order methods save communication at the cost of on-device compute and therefore energy. **No paper quantifies the net joules per accuracy point** against LoRA-FL on the same hardware, so whether the trade pays off on a battery-powered phone is unknown. Second, every family that achieves first-order accuracy at low device memory does so by **sending activations to a server**. The efficiency literature and the privacy literature have not yet been reconciled.

## NPUs solved prefill; decode and memory remain the bottleneck

On-device inference engines shifted in 2024–2025 from CPU/GPU runtimes (llama.cpp, MLC-LLM) to **NPU-centric and heterogeneous engines**. llm.npu (ASPLOS 2025) addresses a specific obstacle: mobile NPUs need static graph shapes, and re-preparing the graph for each prompt length costs about **11 s for Gemma-2B**. It splits prompts into fixed-size chunks, moves outlier activations to the CPU or GPU, and schedules Transformer blocks by hardware affinity. It reports **22.4× faster prefill, 30.7× energy savings, up to 32.8× end-to-end speedup, and more than 1,000 tok/s prefill** for a billion-parameter model ([arXiv 2407.05858](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.05858)). HeteroLLM independently claims more than 1,000 tok/s prefill on Qualcomm hardware through CPU/GPU/NPU co-execution. This comes from a search summary only ([arXiv 2501.14794](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.14794v1)). Alibaba's MNN-LLM, tested on a Xiaomi 14, reports **up to 25.3× prefill and 7.1× decode over llama.cpp** on GPU, and 2.8× and 1.7× over MLC-LLM. Its comparison is confounded, though: MLC-LLM used symmetric quantization while the other engines used asymmetric ([arXiv 2506.10443](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10443v1)).

Decode is still bound by memory bandwidth, and the most striking result shows how much it depends on memory. PowerInfer-2 runs TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B on a OnePlus 12 at **11.68 tok/s with 19 GB of free DRAM, but only 2.13 tok/s with 7 GB**. It claims a 3.94× average speedup over LLM-in-a-Flash and 25.4× over llama.cpp. Different versions of the paper report different multipliers (24.6× and 27.8× in another version) ([arXiv v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06282v3)). At the low-bit extreme, BitNet b1.58 2B4T with bitnet.cpp is the notable 2025 step for CPU-only devices. A third-party table reports **0.4 GB of non-embedding memory, 29 ms/token, and an estimated 0.028 J per inference**, against 2 GB, 48 ms and 0.258 J for LLaMA-3.2-1B. These figures are **not verified against the Microsoft report** ([HF Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/knoxel/bitnet-cpp-explorer/blob/main/app.py); [emelia.io](https://emelia.io/hub/bitnet-1bit-llm-cpu-inference)). For federated learning, this matters because once weights are ternary, the binding memory term becomes **activations and the KV cache**. TierKV targets exactly that with multi-tier KV caching on phones ([arXiv 2609.21172](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.21172)).

## Collaborative inference gains are real but modest: 1.1× to 35%

Device–edge–cloud collaborative inference has settled on two patterns. In **speculative decoding across tiers**, a small model on the device drafts tokens and a large model at the edge or in the cloud verifies them. In **layer partitioning**, the model is sharded across several edge nodes. The reported gains are modest. They depend on network round-trip time and acceptance rate more than on model design.

The Yale edge–cloud system puts the draft model on a **Jetson Nano** and the target model on an **A100**. It reports **up to 35% lower latency** than cloud-only autoregressive decoding, **another 11%** from preemptive drafting driven by the target model's early exits, and a **21% speedup** in a real robot deployment ([TMLR 2025](https://mlanthology.org/tmlr/2025/venkatesha2025tmlr-fast)). DSSD (ICML 2025) splits the *verification* step between device and edge to reduce uplink traffic ([ICML](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/47046)). DSD (Nov 2025) extends speculative decoding to multiple devices with adaptive window control and a simulator, but reports only **up to 1.1× speedup and 9.7% higher throughput** over speculative-decoding baselines ([papers.cool](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2511.21669)). PicoSpec (Mar 2026) runs the edge SLM and cloud LLM concurrently in an asynchronous pipeline ([arXiv 2603.19133](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19133v2)). For partitioning, EdgeShard (IEEE IoT-J 2025) jointly chooses devices and partition points with dynamic programming. It reports **up to 50% lower latency and 2× throughput** for Llama-2 on a real testbed ([arXiv 2405.14371](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.14371)).

A different idea comes from the low-resource notes. A **28M-parameter micro-LM** on an Orange Pi produces the first 4–8 words with **45 ms time-to-first-token** while the cloud generates the rest of the reply ([arXiv 2604.19642](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.19642v1)). This optimizes the latency the user perceives rather than throughput, and it fits naturally with federated personalization of the tiny model on the device.

## Wireless papers optimize five knobs, mostly in simulation

The wireless community (IEEE TWC, GLOBECOM, IoT Magazine) frames federated LLM fine-tuning as a joint optimization problem. The decision variables are typically the **split layer, LoRA rank, pruning or quantization ratio, bandwidth and transmit power**. The objective is usually latency or energy under an accuracy constraint. FedsLLM optimizes subchannels, power, split point and rank together ([arXiv 2504.14667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14667v2)). Pang et al. (2026) jointly optimize pruning rates and bandwidth allocation to minimize latency ([arXiv 2602.01024](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.01024v2)). Wang et al. (Sep 2025) propose a switching-based paradigm for heterogeneous wireless edge devices ([arXiv 2509.19306](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19306v1)). Most of these papers are evaluated in simulation with small models.

True three-tier device–edge–cloud hierarchical training is mostly absent. What exists is **group-based or clustered LoRA aggregation**. HierFedLoRA (USTC, 2025) reports **1.6–4.2% higher accuracy and at least 2.1× faster fine-tuning** than strong baselines. The notes could not confirm from the abstract whether it has an explicit edge-server tier ([arXiv 2503.21213](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.21213)). CooperLLM is the clearest cloud–edge–end training design in the notes, with zeroth-order updates on devices and first-order correction in the cloud ([arXiv 2601.12917](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.12917)). The *Federated Intelligence* overview in IEEE IoT Magazine combines federated fine-tuning with collaborative edge reasoning at the conceptual level ([arXiv 2503.21412](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.21412v1)). A non-peer-reviewed December 2025 survey of more than 200 edge-FL papers covers multi-tier FL but mentions on-device adaptation of foundation models only as future outlook ([Preprints.org](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202512.0118/v1)).

## Production keeps gradients off the phone

Industry deployments contradict the framing of much of the academic literature. **No production system found in the notes runs LLM gradient updates on phones.** Google has the most documented pipeline. More than **20 Gboard language models** have been trained and deployed with DP-FTRL under **ρ-zCDP guarantees between 0.2 and 2**, and all new Gboard LM federated tasks now require DP ([arXiv 2305.18465](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.18465); [ACL Industry 2023](https://research.google/pubs/federated-learning-of-gboard-language-models-with-differential-privacy/)). Google also reports live DP-FL training of an English LM across **millions of keyboard devices** ([arXiv 2403.08100](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.08100)). These are small on-device LMs, not LLMs.

Google's LLM work uses federation to steer data rather than to train the LLM. In 2024, Google showed that pretraining on LLM-generated synthetic data gave **19.0% and 22.8% relative next-word-prediction accuracy gains** over a model trained on a standard public dataset ([arXiv 2404.04360](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.04360)). A July 2025 blog post, which the notes saw **only as a snippet** because the page was blocked, describes DP-FL signals from devices guiding synthetic-data generation. That synthetic data then fine-tunes the **server-side** Gboard Proofread LLM, with a small DP-trained "buttress" model refining it ([Google Research blog](https://www.research.google/blog/synthetic-and-federated-privacy-preserving-domain-adaptation-with-llms-for-mobile-applications/)). Proofread itself launched on Pixel 8 but is **served on TPU v5 in Google Cloud**. The tuned PaLM2-XS reached an **85.56% "good ratio"** on a human-labeled golden set ([ACL 2025](https://preview.aclanthology.org/landing_page/2025.acl-long.19.pdf)). Google researchers also argue for refocusing FL on **verifiable privacy through TEEs** rather than strict on-device computation ([arXiv 2410.08892](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.08892)). Their Confidential Federated Computations system combines TEEs, reproducibly built open-source server binaries, and a ledger of access policies that devices authorize ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/confidential-federated-computations/); [code](https://github.com/google-parfait/confidential-federated-compute)).

Apple's 2025 foundation-model report describes an on-device model of about **3B parameters** with KV-cache sharing and 2-bit quantization-aware training, plus a Parallel-Track MoE server model on Private Cloud Compute ([arXiv 2507.13575](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.13575v2)). Secondary sources disagree on the effective bit-width ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2025/07/apple-foundation-models-ios26); [Substack](https://marklwatson.substack.com/p/apples-hybrid-intelligence-architecture)). The notes did **not verify** any Apple private federated learning or federated evaluation for Apple Intelligence, and they retrieved **nothing on Qualcomm (AI Hub, QNN/Genie, on-device LoRA) or Samsung (Gauss, Galaxy AI)**. Apple's open-source pfl-research simulator ([arXiv 2404.06430](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06430)) and *Mobile Foundation Model as Firmware* (MobiCom 2024, [arXiv 2308.14363](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14363)) are relevant prior-knowledge pointers, both marked **U**.

## Benchmarks are arriving, but none measures joules

Federated LLM benchmarking has matured on the algorithm side but not on the systems side. FlowerTune (NeurIPS 2025 Datasets & Benchmarks) is the main public leaderboard. It covers four domains (general NLP, finance, medical, code) and **26 base models up to about 13–14B**, and it includes tools for measuring communication and memory ([arXiv 2506.02961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02961); [leaderboard](https://flower.ai/benchmarks/llm-leaderboard)). Its practical recipe of 4-bit quantization with LoRA rank 8 and FedProx on Qwen2.5-7B shows that **QLoRA is the de facto baseline**, but it runs in simulation on a single GPU ([Flower app](https://flower.ai/apps/mrs83/flowertune_qwen2.5-7b-instruct-medical)). FedLLM-Bench (NeurIPS 2024 Datasets & Benchmarks) and OpenFedLLM offer realistic federated datasets and include alignment ([NeurIPS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/c8cdab0e890c59255c27977072fdb0f0-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.pdf); [arXiv 2402.06954](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.06954)).

The edge-specific benchmark is **EdgeFlowerTune** (2026). It evaluates FedAvg+LoRA, FedProx+LoRA, HeteroLoRA and SplitLoRA under realistic edge system constraints, using **Gemma-3-270M and Qwen2.5-0.5B** ([arXiv 2605.08636](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08636)). Those model sizes show how far real-edge evaluation lags behind the 7B models on the simulation leaderboards. Energy is the weakest axis. The only physical power study in the notes covers CNN/RNN FL on a Raspberry Pi 4. It found that **communication raised power 8–32% above idle**, and that training averaged about **3.3 W** ([ACM UCC 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3603166.3632545)). EcoFL (2026) estimates energy with a **TDP-based model** and names hardware power-monitor validation as an essential next step ([MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/jlpea16030024)).

## Split activations leak, and that privacy cost goes unpriced

The efficiency papers above treat uploaded activations as a communication cost. The 2026 privacy literature, cross-referenced from the federated-privacy notes, shows that they are also a **data-leakage channel**. A 2026 survey of split learning for LLM fine-tuning documents model-inversion reconstruction of inputs from "smashed data" (the activations sent to the server). It also describes BiSR, a bidirectional attack that combines forward-activation inversion with backward gradient matching ([arXiv 2604.24468](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.24468v1)). PrivPair (2026) shows a **"zero-cost decoder"**: the server simply runs the client's split layers and output head on the activations it receives. **Reconstruction quality improves as fine-tuning proceeds** ([arXiv 2609.09794](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.09794v1)). PIDI recovers both prompts and generated responses from a head-body-tail split LLM at inference time, so collaborative *inference* is exposed too ([arXiv 2606.14210](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.14210)). Of the split-FL efficiency papers, **none reported formal privacy guarantees** in the abstracts the notes saw. For adapter-based FL, DP-FedLoRA adds Gaussian noise to LoRA matrices on devices ([arXiv 2509.09097](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.09097)). LA-LoRA shows that LoRA's semi-quadratic update turns DP noise into a destabilizing non-Gaussian term ([arXiv 2602.19926](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19926v1)). These results mean that low-rank efficiency and DP interact badly, which matters for any edge system meant to ship with formal privacy.

## Ten open problems define the 2026–2027 agenda

The gaps below combine the gaps the notes state explicitly with this report's inferences. Each is phrased so it can become a research question.

The first and most fundamental gap is **scale on real hardware**. Fed MobiLLM tops out at OPT-1.3B on Jetson boards, EdgeFlowerTune uses 270M–0.5B models, and Just Talk Once has phones in its testbed but the notes recorded no model sizes or numbers for it. FwdLLM *claims* billion-parameter FL on commercial phones, but **federated fine-tuning of 3–8B models on physical phones is not shown** in any source found ([Fed MobiLLM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06765); [EdgeFlowerTune](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08636)). The second gap is **energy and thermal accounting**. Among the systems surveyed, only llm.npu reports energy, and that is for inference. No paper measures joules per round or battery drain for federated LLM fine-tuning on a phone, Pi or Jetson. Energy benchmarks still rely on TDP emulation ([EcoFL](https://doi.org/10.3390/jlpea16030024)). So the central trade-off of zeroth-order methods, fewer bytes sent in exchange for many more forward passes, has **never been priced in joules per accuracy point**.

The third gap is **private split learning**. Every first-order, low-memory design uploads activations, and PrivPair shows that leakage grows during training. The unanswered question is how much accuracy and latency DP on smashed data costs at LLM scale. The fourth gap is **aggregation under mixed quantization and mixed rank**. Heterogeneous-rank aggregation is converging on product-space methods (FlexLoRA, FSLoRA, FedPA-LoRA), but no work aggregates adapters trained on backbones quantized *differently* per client (4-bit, 2-bit, 1.58-bit). The notes found **no strong evidence of ternary or 2-bit backbones inside FL fine-tuning** ([FedPA-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.15381)). The fifth gap is **fair engine benchmarking**. MNN-LLM's comparison is confounded by quantization, PowerInfer-2's multipliers differ between versions, and ExecuTorch, LiteRT and Qualcomm's QNN/Genie have no peer-reviewed numbers ([MNN-LLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10443v1); [PowerInfer-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06282v3)).

The sixth gap is **deployed hierarchy**. No three-tier device–edge–cloud LLM FL system has been validated, and the wireless optimization papers are mostly simulations with small models. The seventh is **training and serving designed together**. Collaborative inference uses a draft model on the device, and federated personalization trains a model on the device, but no paper trains the speculative-decoding draft model or micro-LM federatedly while optimizing its acceptance rate against the edge or cloud verifier. The eighth is **connectivity and stragglers**. FedPipe documents severe stragglers among heterogeneous edge servers, and the notes found nothing on asynchronous or delay-tolerant FedLLM for intermittently connected devices ([FedPipe](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.06448)). The ninth is **architecture and tokenizer heterogeneity**. Clients running SmolLM, Qwen or Gemma SLMs can only be federated today through FedMKT-style token alignment, and there is no systematic study. The tenth is **on-device agents**. The agentic-FL notes found no confirmed paper on cross-device FL of personal LLM agents on phones. Edge caching (KV, prompt and semantic caching at MEC servers) and vehicular LLM inference were not searched at all.

## Conclusion

The field's real frontier is not a better optimizer but **where activations live**. Every 2025–2026 design that keeps first-order accuracy at low device memory does so by moving activations to a server, and the same year's privacy research showed those activations can be inverted. A credible edge federated LLM system therefore has to solve efficiency and privacy *together*, for example zeroth-order or side-tuned updates on a quantized SLM, with any smashed data sent only to TEE-attested edge servers. Industry's choice points the same way. Google and Apple have both avoided on-device LLM gradients and invested instead in verifiable server-side privacy and device–cloud routing. That suggests the most deployable near-term "federated LLM at the edge" is federated *signals, adapters and drafts* around a cloud LLM, not federated backpropagation through it.

For a PhD agenda, the highest-leverage contribution is methodological rather than algorithmic. A physical-device benchmark that reports peak RSS, joules per round, rounds-to-target accuracy and bytes per round across zeroth-order, side-tuning, split and heterogeneous-LoRA methods on phones, Jetson and Pi would settle comparisons that currently rest on GPU simulation and self-reported multipliers. It would also likely reorder the literature, because the trade of communication for compute that favors zeroth-order methods on paper has never been tested against a battery.
