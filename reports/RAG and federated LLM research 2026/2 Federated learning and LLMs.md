# Federated LLMs outgrow FedAvg on adapters

Between 2024 and late 2026, federated learning for large language models (FL+LLM) became mostly **federated LoRA**. Its central technical finding is that FedIT-style averaging of the A and B factors separately is mathematically inexact, because the mean of the products B_k·A_k is not the product of the means. Three rival fixes appeared in 2024–2025. **Exact aggregation** covers FLoRA (NeurIPS 2024), FedEx-LoRA (ACL 2025) and Fed-SB. **Asymmetric sharing** covers FedSA-LoRA (ICLR 2025). **Rank reconciliation** covers HetLoRA, FlexLoRA (NeurIPS 2024) and their successors. A further wave of preprints in **June–August 2026** (PreLort, SeFoRA, FedPA-LoRA, raFLoRA, "Rethinking Factor Sharing") shows that no single method has won. Published results also conflict: FLoRA failed to converge in LoRA-FAIR's experiments, and FSLoRA's runs put FlexLoRA about 5 points ahead of FLoRA. The other sub-areas trail federated SFT. **Federated alignment** got its first convergence theory for federated DPO only in 2026, and federated GRPO exists only in FedAgent (ICLR 2026) and preprints. **Federated pretraining** has merged with the DiLoCo low-communication line: Photon trained 7B models federatively (MLSys 2025), DiLoCo scaling laws appeared at NeurIPS 2025, and over-the-internet runs reached 72B in 2026, but almost all of these assume IID data shards rather than siloed non-IID data. **FlowerTune** (NeurIPS 2025 Datasets & Benchmarks) and **FedLLM-Bench** (NeurIPS 2024 D&B) give the field shared testbeds, yet no neutral head-to-head comparison of the main aggregation methods has been published. One caution covers everything below. The underlying research could not open arxiv.org, so **every claim rests on search-result snippets and abstracts, not full-text reading**, and all numbers are the authors' own. The evidence tags in each table mark which claims are thinner still.

## Averaging LoRA factors is wrong, and three fixes compete

### The foundational baseline and why it breaks

**FedIT** ("Towards Building the Federated GPT: Federated Instruction Tuning", Zhang et al.) is the reference point for all later work. Clients download a global LLM, run LoRA instruction tuning on their own instructions, and the server averages the A and B matrices separately ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05644v2); [mechanics as described in arXiv 2509.26399](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.26399)). Its GPT-4 auto-evaluation shows the federated model beating centralized training on limited local instructions alone ([deepai summary](https://deepai.org/publication/towards-building-the-federated-gpt-federated-instruction-tuning)). That is the original case for the whole field: pooling instructions you cannot centralize beats training on your own silo.

The flaw is algebraic. Each client's update is ΔW_k = B_k A_k. FedAvg on the factors returns mean(B)·mean(A), which differs from the true mean(B_k A_k). FedEx-LoRA states the problem as existing methods "rely on traditional federated averaging of LoRA adapters, resulting in inexact updates" ([ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.67/)). FLoRA calls the result "mathematically inaccurate aggregation noise" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.05976)). The error grows with client heterogeneity, because the more the B_k and A_k diverge, the larger the cross terms that factor-wise averaging drops. The 2024–2026 literature splits by how it removes this error and what it pays to do so.

Each exact method moves the cost somewhere else. FLoRA's stacked global adapter has rank equal to the sum of client ranks, so download size and adapter memory grow with the number of clients ([FedPA-LoRA's summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.15381)). FedEx-LoRA keeps adapters small but has to ship a full-size residual correction to the base weights, and it assumes one shared rank. Fed-SB is the most elegant: it makes the trainable object a single r×r matrix, so linear averaging *is* exact. It pays in expressivity, since only R is learned. Our synthesis is that FLoRA's "write the update into the base model and re-initialize" step is exactly the part LoRA-FAIR blames for non-convergence. "Exact" aggregation is therefore not free of optimization side-effects.

FedSA-LoRA and FFA-LoRA make **opposite choices about the same factor**. FedSA shares A and keeps B private. FFA freezes A and shares B. Both remove the cross-term error by fixing or localizing one factor. FedSA's empirical claim that A carries general knowledge makes its design a natural personalized-FL method. FFA's motivation comes from differential privacy (see the privacy section below). LoRA-A² sits between them by alternating which factor is frozen. The August 2026 "Rethinking Factor Sharing" paper shows the question is still open ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.09742v1)). This is our synthesis, not a sourced claim: no paper in the notes tests whether FedSA's "A is general" finding survives DP noise, quantized backbones or very non-IID splits.

## Rank heterogeneity spawned a dozen 2026 aggregation papers

When clients have different memory budgets they choose different LoRA ranks, and aggregation then has to reconcile matrices of different shapes. This is the busiest sub-area of 2025–2026, and its taxonomy is now stable. **Zero-pad and truncate** (HetLoRA) is simple but biased. **SVD redistribution of the full product** (FlexLoRA) is accurate but costs server compute. **Stacking** (FLoRA) is exact but grows with K. **Sketching, product-space and nested-rank** schemes are the 2025–2026 refinements ([low-resource notes summary via FedPA-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.15381); [FSLoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19389v4)).

Three patterns stand out. First, **the field now treats initialization error and aggregation error as one problem**. LoRA-FAIR, ILoRA and FedPA-LoRA all name both in their titles or framing: what the client restarts from after aggregation matters as much as what the server computes. Second, the low-resource notes argue that rank-heterogeneous design is **converging on product-space (ΔW) aggregation with a decoupled communication rank**, a line running FlexLoRA → FSLoRA → FedPA-LoRA ([FedPA-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.15381)). That is an inference from mechanisms, not a measured consensus. Third, **the evidence on FLoRA directly conflicts**. FLoRA's authors present stacking as noise-free. LoRA-FAIR reports FLoRA failing to converge ([arXiv 2411.14961](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.14961)). FSLoRA's experiments put FLoRA 2–5 points behind FlexLoRA ([arXiv 2501.19389](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19389v4)). These are all competitor-run comparisons with competitor-chosen hyperparameters. A PhD reader should treat the ranking as unresolved, not as evidence against stacking.

One more structural point. The low-resource notes stress that rank heterogeneity helps only clients that can already hold the frozen backbone in memory. Below roughly 4 GB of device memory, the realistic designs are the ones in the next section ([low-resource synthesis drawing on FedMKT](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.17/)).

## Below LoRA: zeroth-order, layer allocation and SLM proxies for weak clients

### Zeroth-order and forward-only tuning trade steps for kilobytes

Zeroth-order (ZO) methods estimate gradients from forward passes alone. This brings client memory down to inference level and, with shared random seeds, brings communication down to kilobytes. The price is many more steps and sensitivity to hyperparameters. The 2025–2026 ZO work has moved past "does it converge" to fixing sample efficiency and non-IID drift, and towards hybrid schemes in which the cloud corrects the ZO gradient.

### Model heterogeneity is handled by distillation, and LLMs now help FL

When clients run *different* architectures or tokenizers, adapters cannot be averaged at all. The answer so far is knowledge distillation between a server LLM and client small language models (SLMs). In the reverse direction, LLMs and foundation models have started to serve FL itself, generating synthetic data that evens out non-IID clients.

## Federated alignment trails federated SFT by roughly two years

Federated instruction tuning dates from 2023. Federated preference alignment only matured in 2026. **FedPDPO** (arXiv 2603.19741, March 2026) reports that vanilla DPO degrades in FL under non-IID preferences and that its implicit rewards generalize poorly. It responds with a personalized federated DPO framework and reports **up to +4.80% average accuracy** over baselines ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19741)). Two months later, **"Distributed Direct Preference Optimization"** (arXiv 2605.20696) claimed the **first convergence and complexity analysis of DPO in both federated and decentralized settings**, citing FedPDPO as concurrent empirical work ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.20696)). That timeline is the clearest signal of the gap: two years after FedIT, the theory of federated DPO was still being written.

The notes found no work on *preserving* a base model's alignment through federated SFT rounds. For a field whose clients are by definition untrusted, that gap stands out.

## Federated pretraining merged with the DiLoCo low-communication line

Federated pretraining and "low-communication distributed training" are now algorithmically the same family: local SGD with an inner optimizer (AdamW or Muon) and an outer optimizer (Nesterov or error feedback) applied to pseudo-gradients every H steps. The difference is framing. **Photon** is the explicitly FL-framed system, aimed at cross-silo data. The DiLoCo descendants are framed around data centres or permissionless compute, with data shards that are effectively IID. Our synthesis is that the real distinction is therefore *whether data is siloed and non-IID*, not the optimizer.

## Sharing only adapters no longer counts as privacy

Privacy was outside the scope of the primary notes, but the privacy notes contain findings that bear directly on the aggregation designs above, so they are summarized here. **MineGrad** (arXiv 2608.01521, Aug 2026) is a malicious-server attack that reconstructs fine-tuning data analytically from shared LoRA gradients. It works for language and vision tasks, and it works even when the number of tokens exceeds the LoRA rank ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.01521)). **ICP-MIA** (NDSS 2026) reports that **higher LoRA rank increases membership-inference success**, while 4-bit QLoRA appears to reduce it ([NDSS PDF](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2026-f892-paper.pdf)). On the defense side, the FFA-LoRA observation that **DP noise is amplified through the B·A product** started a 2025–2026 line of work. **FedASK** (NeurIPS 2025) uses double sketching to update both factors under DP, though an independent review questions its two central theorems ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09990); [Pith review](https://pith.science/paper/2507.09990)). **LA-LoRA** (2026) formalizes the semi-quadratic update that turns Gaussian DP noise into a non-Gaussian quadratic term, and remedies it with alternating updates and a low-pass filter ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19926v1)). FedShield-LLM combines LoRA, pruning and CKKS homomorphic encryption for encrypted aggregation ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.05640)).

What follows is our synthesis, not a sourced claim. The privacy results and the aggregation results have been developed largely in isolation. FedEx-LoRA's full-size residual, FLoRA's growing stacked rank and FlexLoRA's server-side full ΔW each change what an attacker or a DP mechanism sees. Rank, which the heterogeneity papers treat as a pure resource knob, is also a privacy knob according to ICP-MIA. None of the notes report an aggregation method evaluated jointly for exactness, communication *and* DP utility.

Two further gaps are narrower. First, **asynchrony and stragglers in federated fine-tuning** are thin. The notes found a 2024 paper that handles stragglers through automated per-client configuration ([arXiv 2404.06448](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.06448)), but no staleness-aware asynchronous federated LoRA method with quantitative speedups. On the pretraining side, overlap gains saturate at about five steps ([arXiv 2606.22768](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22768)). Second, **server optimizers at LLM scale** (FedAdam/FedYogi, SCAFFOLD-style control variates) have no 2025–2026 LLM-scale evidence against a well-tuned DiLoCo outer Nesterov step. Recent gains came from rethinking the outer state, momentum versus error feedback, rather than from classic drift correction ([SparseLoCo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15706)).

## Conclusion

The defining shift of 2025–2026 is that federated LLM research stopped treating the adapter as an opaque payload to average and started treating its *factorization* as the object of study. Once the field accepted that mean(BA) ≠ mean(B)·mean(A), every design choice became a choice about which factor is shared, frozen, stacked, sketched or corrected. That one lens now connects aggregation exactness, personalization (FedSA's "A is general"), privacy (FFA's DP noise amplification) and resource heterogeneity (rank reconciliation). Treating these four as one problem is where we expect the next strong theory paper to come from. A method that is exact, rank-flexible, cheap and DP-stable all at once has not been shown to exist, and showing why it cannot, if that is the case, would itself be a contribution.

The second implication is about where FL+LLM is heading. In pretraining, algorithms built by teams without data-privacy constraints (DiLoCo, SparseLoCo, DeMo) now set the state of the art, and FL's distinctive contribution shrinks to the *non-IID, siloed-data* assumption, which those methods have not tested. In alignment, agentic and decentralized-RL groups (FedAgent, INTELLECT-2) are moving faster than the classic FL community. Federated LLM research therefore keeps its edge only by owning the parts that the others ignore: statistical heterogeneity, formal privacy and trust between institutions. Any claim in this report tagged T, 2° or C should be checked against the full paper before it is cited in your own work.
