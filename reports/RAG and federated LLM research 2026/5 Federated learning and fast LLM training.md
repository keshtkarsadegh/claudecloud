# Outer optimizers and sparse pseudo-gradients shrink LLM training traffic

Between 2024 and 2026, fast and communication-efficient federated and decentralized LLM training settled on one recipe. Workers run a DiLoCo-style local-SGD loop (an inner AdamW or Muon optimizer for tens to hundreds of steps). An outer optimizer then applies a Nesterov or error-feedback update to a pseudo-gradient that is compressed to int8, or to 2-bit values at 1–3% density, and that sync is increasingly overlapped with compute. The strongest evidence that this is more than "worse but cheaper" is the NeurIPS 2025 DiLoCo scaling-law paper: tuned DiLoCo matched or beat data-parallel training at 4B and 10B parameters with more than 100× less total communication ([NeurIPS 2025](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/99acb4c087266e80b547aed79247266b-Abstract-Conference.html)). On the engineering side, error-feedback compression (DeMo up to 85×, SparseLoCo at 97–99% sparsity with 2-bit values) took over-the-internet pretraining from a whitelisted 10B run (INTELLECT-1, December 2024) to a permissionless 72B run (Covenant-72B, March 2026) ([arXiv:2603.08163](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.08163)). Federated fine-tuning has a different bottleneck. LoRA payloads are already small, so 2025–2026 work there is about making adapter aggregation exact under heterogeneous ranks (FedEx-LoRA, FLoRA, FlexLoRA, FedPA-LoRA). Zeroth-order methods such as FedKSeed push traffic down to under 18 KB per round, but pay for it in many more steps. The headline "×" figures do not compare directly: they measure bandwidth, per-GPU bytes, throughput, sync latency or wall time against different baselines, and the clearest case (DiLoCoX's 357× throughput) came with a worse final loss. The biggest open problems are a bandwidth-parameterized time-to-target-loss benchmark, scaling laws for the compressed and gossip variants, theory for why error feedback can replace outer momentum, and moving the IID-shard results to genuinely siloed, non-IID federated data.

**How to read the evidence.** This report synthesizes research notes gathered on 2026-10-30. Direct fetching of arxiv.org failed in the research sandbox, so every arXiv-derived figure was read from search-engine snippets of the abstract or HTML page, not from the full PDF. Tables carry an evidence code. **A** means the number appears on a primary page: a proceedings abstract, the arXiv abstract or HTML, or the authors' own blog or research page. **A\*** means the same abstract text reached through a mirror or aggregator (papers.cool, alphaXiv, Hugging Face Papers). **S** means the claim comes only from secondary sources: news, third-party reviews, crypto-media or paper-notes sites. **U** means unverified: title-only, idea-only without numbers, conflicting, or flagged in the notes as recalled from prior knowledge. **(I)** marks my own arithmetic or inference. Treat every number as the authors' claim until it is checked against the PDF.

## DiLoCo now scales as well as data parallelism at a fraction of the bandwidth

DiLoCo's core idea is to replace per-step gradient all-reduce with an outer step every H inner steps. That has become the default for cross-datacenter and over-the-internet pretraining, and 2025 is when Google's group turned it from a heuristic into something predictable. The scaling-law paper by Charles, Douillard and colleagues fits laws for evaluation loss and for optimal hyperparameters under a fixed compute budget. It finds that **well-tuned DiLoCo scales better than data-parallel (DP) training as model size grows, beats DP even at small sizes, and has a larger optimal global batch size** ([HF Papers](https://huggingface.co/papers/2503.09799); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09799v1)). The larger batch size matters in practice: a method that tolerates bigger batches has fewer sequential steps to synchronize. The paper extrapolates its laws to 4B and 10B parameters, where DiLoCo beats DP while cutting **total communication by more than 100×** ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09799v1)). This is the only DiLoCo-family result framed as equal-or-better quality at equal compute rather than as a communication saving, and it anchors the rest of the area.

The other Google work in 2025 attacked the remaining cost, which is the blocking all-reduce of a full model-sized pseudo-gradient every H steps. **Streaming DiLoCo** syncs parameter *fragments* on a staggered schedule, which lowers peak bandwidth. It also overlaps the sync with continued inner computation and quantizes the outer gradients ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.18512v1)). Secondary write-ups describe roughly two orders of magnitude less bandwidth in 1B/10B/100B simulations, with gains growing with scale ([emergentmind](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2501.18512); [Import AI 398](https://jack-clark.net/2025/02/03/import-ai-398-deepmind-makes-distributed-training-better-ai-versus-the-intelligence-community-and-another-chinese-reasoning-model/)). The primary abstract's exact figure was not retrieved. **Eager Updates** (Kale, Douillard, Donchev) handles the in-flight period differently: each worker applies its own local outer gradient as a proxy until the global all-reduce lands, and the authors report this is competitive with standard DiLoCo at low bandwidth ([arXiv:2502.12996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12996)). The limits of overlap became clearer in 2026. An independent re-evaluation, Factored Gossip DiLoCo, notes that Streaming DiLoCo itself recommends **at most about 5 steps of overlap** before quality drops. It reports that the overlap gives **negligible compute-utilization gains in the low-bandwidth regime it targets**, and proposes a gossip variant instead ([arXiv:2606.22768](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22768)). This finding comes from a search snippet and is unverified. A resilience-oriented "Decoupled DiLoCo" was also posted in 2026 ([arXiv:2604.21428](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.21428)), but only its title was confirmed.

## Error feedback lets pseudo-gradients travel at 1–3% density

With H inner steps already cutting sync frequency by about two orders of magnitude, the next gains came from compressing *what* is sent. Across groups, the answer was **error feedback**: send only the top components and keep the residual locally so nothing is permanently lost. Nous Research's **DeMo** (ICLR 2026) does this at the optimizer level. Each node keeps a local, unsynchronized momentum, takes a DCT, and transmits only the top-k fast-moving components while the residual stays in the momentum buffer. On 300M and 1B models it reports **up to 85× less data per GPU than AdamW-DDP at comparable loss and accuracy** ([ICLR 2026](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/17790fc9b4828206f99a0dea35da3657-Abstract-Conference.html)). Templar/Mila's **SparseLoCo** brings the same idea to DiLoCo pseudo-gradients. It combines top-k sparsification with **2-bit quantization at 97–99% sparsity** and replaces DiLoCo's outer Nesterov momentum with a single error-feedback accumulator, which the authors argue approximates outer momentum locally ([arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15706v2)). Its ablations find top-k beats random-k and 2-bit quantization costs almost nothing ([arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15706v1)). It reports outperforming DiLoCo on dense models from 178M to 2B and on a 645M-total/273M-active MoE ([alphaXiv](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.15706)). A 2026 v3 exists and was not checked. **MuLoCo** adds an optimizer-side finding: with **Muon as the inner optimizer**, DiLoCo becomes robust enough to compression that 2-bit quantization with error feedback **matches or beats uncompressed DiLoCo while communicating 8× less** ([papers.cool abstract](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2505.23725)).

Read together, these three papers say something stronger than "compression works". SparseLoCo's result, that an error-feedback buffer can *replace* outer momentum rather than sit beside it, suggests the outer optimizer state and the compression residual are the same object. No paper in the notes proves this. It is the cleanest theoretical gap in the area (see the gaps section).

A parallel line removes global collectives altogether. Gensyn's **NoLoCo** replaces the all-reduce with partial pairwise averaging against a random peer inside a Nesterov-momentum variant. With up to 1,000 internet GPUs and models from 125M to 6.8B, it reports **about 10× lower sync latency than DiLoCo-style all-reduce and about 4% faster convergence** to the same validation loss ([Gensyn research](https://www.gensyn.ai/research/noloco-training-large-models-with-no-all-reduce); [HF Papers](https://huggingface.co/papers/2506.10911)). **GASLoC** (2026) unifies local communication with local updates and claims to clearly outperform DiLoCo under heterogeneous bandwidth. Its numbers were not verified ([arXiv:2606.11081](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.11081)). At the systems end, 0G Labs' **DiLoCoX** trains Qwen1.5-107B over 1 Gbps links. It combines pipeline parallelism, a dual optimizer policy, one-step-delay overlap and adaptive compression to reach **3,728 tokens/s against 10.4 tokens/s for AllReduce (357×) and 1.35× CocktailSGD**. Its ablations drop to 2,197 tokens/s without overlap and 1,168 tokens/s without compression. The catch is that **AllReduce reaches a lower loss (3.90 against 4.20)** ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21263v1)). **DES-LOC** syncs parameters and Adam moments at different frequencies, but its quantitative claims could not be retrieved ([arXiv:2505.22549](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22549)).

## Internet-scale runs grew from 10B to 72B parameters in fifteen months

Public, over-the-internet runs show where these algorithms meet real networks, untrusted participants and economics. Prime Intellect's **INTELLECT-1** (technical report, December 2024) trained a 10B model across continents with DiLoCo. It synced every **100 inner steps** with **int8 pseudo-gradients**, the outer all-reduce took under a minute (**1–2% of training time**), and int8 had no visible effect on the loss curves ([PI blog](https://primeintellect.ai/blog/intellect-1-release); [PI blog](https://primeintellect.ai/blog/intellect-1)). Weights are public ([HF](https://huggingface.co/PrimeIntellect/INTELLECT-1-fp32)). Its reported bandwidth reduction is inconsistent: the blog says **400×** against DP and the arXiv report says **"up to 2000×"** ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.01152v1)). **INTELLECT-2** (May 2025) changed the workload to globally distributed RL for a 32B reasoning model. prime-rl decouples rollout generation, training and weight broadcast with two-step asynchrony. SHARDCAST distributes weights, and TOPLOC verifies rollouts from untrusted workers ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07291v1); [PI blog](https://primeintellect.ai/blog/intellect-2-release)). It improved over QwQ-32B on math and code with a slight IFEval drop ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/PrimeIntellect/INTELLECT-2/blob/refs%2Fpr%2F9/README.md)). The often-quoted 83% global and 96% US-only compute utilization comes from a secondary write-up ([learnopoly](https://learnopoly.com/primeinintellect-liberates-intellect-2-a-32b-reasoning-model-formed-via-learning-to-strengthen-asynchronous-distributed/)).

Nous Research's **Psyche** network implements DisTrO/DeMo in Rust with P2P networking. Its first run, **Consilience**, targets a **40B MLA-architecture model on 20T tokens** ([Nous Psyche](https://nousresearch.com/nous-psyche/)), with whitelisted participants according to the Covenant-72B paper ([arXiv:2603.08163](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.08163)). Its status as of October 2026 is unconfirmed. Templar/Covenant AI's **Covenant-72B** (arXiv, March 2026) is the first run described as **permissionless**, on Bittensor Subnet 3 ([arXiv:2603.08163](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.08163)). Its outcome numbers come from secondary sources. Those sources report completion on March 10, 2026 with more than 70 nodes, **67.1 zero-shot MMLU**, results ahead of LLaMA-2-70B and LLM360 K2 under identical evaluation, **more than 146× SparseLoCo compression against dense gradients**, and **about 70 s of communication per round against an estimated 8.3 min for INTELLECT-1** ([BlockEden](https://blockeden.xyz/blog/2026/03/13/templar-covenant-72b-bittensor-largest-decentralized-llm-pretraining/); [KuCoin news](https://www.kucoin.com/news/flash/bittensor-subnet-completes-largest-decentralized-llm-pre-training-with-covenant-72b)). Two single-source reports raise doubts about the long-term viability of decentralized runs. One says INTELLECT-3 was trained on a centralized 512-GPU cluster ([implicator.ai](https://www.implicator.ai/prime-intellects-intellect-3-open-source-ambition-meets-centralized-reality/)). The other says Covenant AI left Bittensor on April 10, 2026 ([ownyourmind.ai](https://ownyourmind.ai/tokenomics/templar-bittensor-covenant-exit/)). Neither was checked against primary materials.

### Photon is the one system that frames pretraining as federated learning

Most DiLoCo-family results shard IID data across compute islands. **Photon** (Flower Labs and Cambridge, MLSys 2025) instead treats pretraining as cross-silo FL, with each client holding its own data source. It trains models up to **7B**, reports **better perplexity than centralized pretraining**, **about 2× faster convergence to target perplexity than DiLoCo** (η=0.1, across three client counts), and **35% lower wall time than baseline distributed training while communicating 64×–512× less** ([MLSys 2025](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/185087ea328b4f03ea8fd0c8aa96f747-Abstract-Conference.html); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.02908v1)). Its convergence edge comes partly from **small client batches with high learning rates**, which federated averaging tolerates ([arXiv:2411.02908](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.02908)). That sits in interesting tension with the DiLoCo scaling-law finding that DiLoCo prefers a *larger* global batch than DP. Code is at [github.com/relogu/photon](https://github.com/relogu/photon) ([Flower blog](https://flower.ai/blog/2025-05-09-photon)). The same group's position paper argues that LLM pretraining's future is federated ([arXiv:2405.10853](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10853)).

## Headline multipliers measure five different things

To compare these results quantitatively, the first step is to admit that the "×" figures are not in the same unit. Table 4 normalizes them by **what was measured** and **against what**. The pattern is clear. Only the scaling-law paper, Photon and NoLoCo report something close to **time or compute to a target loss**. DeMo and MuLoCo report bytes at comparable loss. DiLoCoX reports raw throughput, and its quality is worse. The Covenant and INTELLECT-2 operating figures are secondary.

Two pieces of arithmetic make the table easier to read. First, INTELLECT-1's **400× matches the product of its two levers exactly**: syncing every 100 steps instead of every step is 100×, and int8 instead of fp32 is 4× ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01152v1)). The "up to 2000×" figure would correspond to roughly H=500 at int8. That reading is mine (I) and is not stated by Prime Intellect. Second, it shows why 72B needed sparsity. A dense bf16 pseudo-gradient for 72B parameters is about 144 GB per sync. At >146× compression that falls to roughly 1 GB (I), about 8 s of transfer at 1 Gbps (I), which is consistent with the reported roughly 70 s communication rounds once aggregation and validation are included. int8 alone would leave about 72 GB (I). The nominal SparseLoCo ratio (1–3% density × 2-bit against 32-bit) would be several hundred times. That the reported end-to-end figure is ">146×" suggests index-encoding and metadata overheads take a large share (I, unverified).

The practical conclusion for a researcher is to report **tokens/s/GPU at a fixed target loss for a specified bandwidth and latency profile**, or bytes per token to target loss, and to never report compression ratio or throughput alone. DiLoCoX shows how a 357× throughput result can sit next to a worse final model ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21263v1)).

## Federated fine-tuning trades bytes for exactness and steps

In cross-silo fine-tuning, LoRA already shrinks per-round traffic to O(r(m+n)) per layer, so the speed problem moves from bandwidth to **convergence lost to biased aggregation**. The underlying issue is that mean(B_k A_k) ≠ mean(B_k)·mean(A_k). FedIT-style factor averaging therefore injects noise that grows with non-IID data and heterogeneous ranks ([ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.67/); [FLoRA arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.05976)).

### Exact and rank-heterogeneous LoRA aggregation became the main battleground

Three families emerged. **Exact aggregation**: FedEx-LoRA folds the residual avg(BA) − avg(B)·avg(A) into the frozen base weights ([ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.67/)). FLoRA stacks client factors so that the sum of products is exact, at the cost of a global rank equal to the sum of client ranks ([NeurIPS 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/28312c9491d60ed0c77f7fff4ad86dd1-Abstract.html)). Fed-SB trains only a small r×r matrix, which makes averaging exact *and* uses **45–90× fewer parameters than LoRA** ([arXiv:2502.15436](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.15436)). **Selective sharing**: FedSA-LoRA (ICLR 2025) observes that A learns general knowledge and B client-specific knowledge, so it uploads only A ([ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/f53a37f820d5be5930415d964f4a0187-Abstract-Conference.html)). That roughly halves upload traffic (I, from the structure, not a reported number). **Rank reconciliation**: FlexLoRA aggregates the full product and redistributes per-client ranks by SVD, gaining about **+2.14% on average** ([arXiv:2402.11505](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11505)). FSLoRA's head-to-head puts **FlexLoRA at 77.90 against FLoRA's 72.81** under a normal rank distribution ([arXiv:2501.19389](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19389v4)). FedPA-LoRA (August 2026) aggregates in product space and **decouples local compute rank from communication rank** ([arXiv:2608.15381](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.15381)). raFLoRA argues that HetLoRA-style zero-padding causes rank collapse ([arXiv:2602.13486](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13486)).

The evidence conflicts in ways that matter for anyone picking a baseline. LoRA-FAIR reports that **FLoRA failed to converge** in its experiments ([arXiv:2411.14961](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.14961)). But LoRA-FAIR's experiments appear to be mainly computer vision, and its authors list moving beyond CV as future work ([arXiv:2411.14961](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.14961)), so the failure has not been shown for LLM fine-tuning. The only hard speed number in this sub-area comes from hierarchical grouping: HierFedLoRA reports **at least 2.1× fine-tuning speedup and +1.6–4.2% accuracy** ([arXiv:2503.21213](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.21213)). EcoLoRA (EMNLP 2025) reports **up to 79% less communication time and 65% less total training time**, but that figure was surfaced only through a snippet attached to a different arXiv entry, and EcoLoRA's own primary link was not retrieved ([snippet source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14667v2)).

### Zeroth-order methods win on bytes and lose on steps

Zeroth-order (ZO) federated tuning sits at the extreme of the communication axis. **FedKSeed** (ICML 2024) does full-parameter ZO tuning over a finite pool of K random seeds. Clients exchange only seeds and scalar projected gradients, which gives **under 18 KB per round, more than 1000× less communication, and inference-level memory** ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/qin24a.html); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.06353)). FedKSeed-Pro samples seeds with different probabilities to need fewer of them, and the authors warn that too large a learning rate diverges ([arXiv:2312.06353](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.06353)). **FedMeZO** supplies the first convergence analysis of MeZO in FL, with a theory-informed personalized learning rate ([arXiv:2402.05926](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05926v3)). Its KDD 2024 venue is unverified. The 2025–2026 follow-ups target ZO's sample inefficiency rather than its bytes. FedSPZO reports **up to 3× lower computation** than other ZO-FL methods up to LLaMA-3.2-3B, while conceding it still trails first-order LoRA ([arXiv:2502.10239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10239)). Meerkat uses transferable extreme sparsity against non-IID drift ([arXiv:2506.03337](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03337v1)). An ACL 2026 paper adds adaptive momentum to stabilize ZO-FL ([ACL 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1851.pdf)). CooperLLM corrects device-side ZO with cloud first-order gradients ([arXiv:2601.12917](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.12917)). Forward-only training is a close relative. FwdLLM (USENIX ATC 2024) reports up to **three orders of magnitude faster convergence than prior backprop-free baselines and 4.6× lower memory** ([USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc24/presentation/xu-mengwei)). Its 2026 journal extension, FwdLLM+, reports **up to 151× faster training and 93% less memory than backprop-based FedLLM** ([IEEE TMC](https://www.computer.org/csdl/journal/tm/2026/08/11429542/2eOD933hNSw)).

The trade-off comes down to which resource is scarce. ZO needs many forward passes per useful update ([Fed MobiLLM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06765)). For cross-silo sites on decent links, LoRA-family methods should therefore win on wall-clock time, and ZO wins only when uplinks are extremely slow or metered, or device memory forbids backpropagation (I). No paper in the notes measures energy or time-to-accuracy for ZO and LoRA on the same physical hardware, so this crossover is uncharacterized. Black-box prompt methods push further. FedBPT tunes prompts gradient-free against an inference API ([arXiv:2310.03123](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.03123)), and FedOne (ICML 2025) shows that activating a single client per round maximizes query efficiency ([ICML](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/poster/45310)).

## Convergence acceleration lives in the outer optimizer, not in drift correction

At LLM scale, the design space has collapsed into the FedOpt-style **inner/outer decomposition**, and the 2025–2026 gains come from rethinking the *outer state*. Classic DiLoCo pairs an AdamW inner optimizer with a Nesterov outer step. MuLoCo switches the inner optimizer to Muon for compression robustness ([papers.cool](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2505.23725)). SparseLoCo replaces outer momentum with error feedback ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15706)). DeMo decouples momentum per node ([ICLR 2026](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/17790fc9b4828206f99a0dea35da3657-Abstract-Conference.html)). DES-LOC desynchronizes parameter and optimizer-state sync ([arXiv:2505.22549](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22549)). NoLoCo embeds gossip averaging inside Nesterov momentum ([HF Papers](https://huggingface.co/papers/2506.10911)). Notably, the notes contain **no 2025–2026 LLM-scale evidence that SCAFFOLD- or FedProx-style control variates beat a well-tuned outer Nesterov step**. The classic FL drift-correction toolbox is largely absent from the frontier. FedProx survives mainly as a FlowerTune baseline, for example the 4-bit QLoRA, rank-8, FedProx recipe for Qwen2.5-7B ([Flower app](https://flower.ai/apps/mrs83/flowertune_qwen2.5-7b-instruct-medical)).

Batch size and learning rate turn out to be first-class convergence levers, and the two strongest papers point in different directions. The DiLoCo scaling laws find a **larger** optimal global batch than DP ([NeurIPS 2025](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/99acb4c087266e80b547aed79247266b-Abstract-Conference.html)). Photon attributes its about 2× edge over DiLoCo to **small local batches with high learning rates** ([arXiv:2411.02908](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.02908)). These are reconcilable, since a small per-client batch times many clients can still be a large global batch (I), but no paper in the notes runs the controlled comparison. Asynchrony helps less than intuition suggests: overlap saturates at about 5 steps for Streaming DiLoCo, and an independent study found negligible utilization gains ([arXiv:2606.22768](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22768)). DiLoCoX's one-step-delay overlap still lifted throughput from 2,197 to 3,728 tokens/s at 107B ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21263v1)), which is consistent with overlap paying off only when sync time is comparable to per-round compute (I). The most mature asynchrony is in RL post-training, where prime-rl's two-step asynchrony decouples rollout, training and broadcast ([arXiv:2505.07291](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07291v1)). For federated fine-tuning, the straggler problem caused by heterogeneous edge servers is documented ([arXiv:2404.06448](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.06448)), but no staleness-aware asynchronous federated LLM fine-tuning method with quantitative speedups turned up.

## Benchmarks cover federated fine-tuning but not decentralized pretraining

For federated fine-tuning there is now a peer-reviewed common testbed. **FlowerTune** (NeurIPS 2025 Datasets & Benchmarks) covers four domains (general NLP on alpaca-gpt4, finance on fingpt-sentiment, medical flashcards, code-alpaca-20k) and 26 base models. It ships model-, method- and aggregation-agnostic templates with communication and memory instrumentation ([arXiv:2506.02961](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02961); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02961v2)), and its live leaderboard has accepted entries since October 2024 ([leaderboard](https://flower.ai/benchmarks/llm-leaderboard)). OpenFedLLM and FedLLM-Bench (NeurIPS 2024 D&B) remain the easiest place to prototype aggregation and alignment algorithms ([arXiv:2402.06954](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.06954); [GitHub](https://github.com/rui-ye/FedLLM-Bench)). FedLLM-Factory implements 15+ federated fine-tuning baselines ([GitHub](https://github.com/boyi-liu/FedLLM-Factory)). EdgeFlowerTune (2026) adds realistic edge constraints, using small models such as Gemma-3-270M and Qwen2.5-0.5B ([arXiv:2605.08636](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08636)). On the industrial side, NVIDIA FLARE supports PEFT and SFT of foundation models ([arXiv:2402.07792](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.07792)), and Flower apps run unchanged on the FLARE runtime ([arXiv:2407.00031](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.00031)).

Decentralized pretraining has **no shared benchmark at all**, and each team reports its own metric. Prime Intellect reports sync-time share and utilization, DiLoCoX tokens/s, NoLoCo sync latency, and Covenant seconds of communication per round. Split federated learning, the other systems lever, mostly targets edge and wireless settings. FedsLLM jointly optimizes split point, LoRA rank and radio resources to minimize delay ([arXiv:2504.14667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14667v2)). A memory-efficient split-FL design reports 79% memory and 6% training-time reductions, but a reviewer notes the memory number comes from BERT-base ([arXiv:2506.02940](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02940)). Split designs carry a privacy cost that compression papers ignore: PrivPair shows a "zero-cost decoder" that reconstructs inputs from smashed activations ([arXiv:2609.09794](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.09794v1)).

## Ten research gaps worth a thesis chapter

The gaps below are synthesized from what the cited papers do and do not show. Each is paired with the evidence that exposes it.

**The field lacks a time-to-target-loss benchmark parameterized by bandwidth and latency.** Table 4 shows ten incompatible units. A benchmark that fixes a model, a token budget and a target loss, and then sweeps link bandwidth (say 100 Mbps to 100 Gbps) and RTT, would make DiLoCo, Streaming, SparseLoCo, NoLoCo and DiLoCoX comparable for the first time. DiLoCoX's throughput-against-loss result ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21263v1)) and the 400×/2000× INTELLECT-1 discrepancy ([blog](https://primeintellect.ai/blog/intellect-1-release); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.01152v1)) show what goes wrong without one. Equivalent pressure exists in FL: the 2026 Springer survey names the lack of standardized benchmarks as a core challenge ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10994-026-07142-7)).

**Scaling laws exist only for uncompressed DiLoCo.** The NeurIPS 2025 laws are validated to about 10B ([NeurIPS](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/99acb4c087266e80b547aed79247266b-Abstract-Conference.html)). SparseLoCo's paper stops at 2B dense and a 645M MoE ([alphaXiv](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.15706)), and NoLoCo at 6.8B ([Gensyn](https://www.gensyn.ai/research/noloco-training-large-models-with-no-all-reduce)). Covenant-72B is a single run, not a law. Joint laws over compression ratio, sync interval H and replica count M would tell practitioners whether compression's quality cost grows or shrinks with scale.

**No theory explains why error feedback can replace outer momentum.** SparseLoCo's empirical claim ([arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15706v2)) and DeMo's decoupled momentum ([ICLR 2026](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/17790fc9b4828206f99a0dea35da3657-Abstract-Conference.html)) point to a unified view of outer optimizer state and compression residual. A convergence analysis in the local-SGD-with-outer-optimizer framework, covering top-k plus quantization with error feedback, is a well-posed and open problem. So is an explanation for why Muon makes compression more forgiving ([MuLoCo](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2505.23725)).

**Extreme compression has not been tested on non-IID federated data.** Photon is the explicitly federated pretraining system ([MLSys](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/185087ea328b4f03ea8fd0c8aa96f747-Abstract-Conference.html)), while the 97–99%-sparse methods assume IID shards on rented compute. Whether error-feedback buffers stay stable under client-specific data distributions, partial participation and client dropout is untested. Pretraining beyond about 10B on truly siloed data is also undemonstrated ([Photon, 7B maximum](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.02908)).

**Overlap and asynchrony saturate early, and nobody has mapped where.** Streaming overlap degrades past about 5 steps, and an independent study saw negligible utilization gains ([arXiv:2606.22768](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22768)). Yet DiLoCoX's one-step delay gave 1.7× throughput (I) ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21263v1)). A model of when overlap pays, as a function of the sync-to-compute time ratio, staleness and the outer learning rate, is missing. So is any staleness-aware asynchronous federated LLM fine-tuning method with measured speedups.

**MoE and RL post-training are almost unexplored for low-communication training.** SparseLoCo has one small MoE result ([alphaXiv](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.15706)), and FoMoE is title-only ([arXiv:2606.19025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19025)). In RL, the bottleneck moves from gradients to **weight broadcast to inference workers** (SHARDCAST) and **verification of untrusted rollouts** (TOPLOC) ([arXiv:2505.07291](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07291v1)). Compressing that broadcast, for example with delta or sparse weight updates, is an open systems problem. Federated DPO convergence theory only appeared in 2026 ([arXiv:2605.20696](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.20696)).

**Rank-heterogeneous LoRA aggregation is still contested.** The 2026 papers (raFLoRA, PreLort, FedPA-LoRA, and SeFoRA, which is title-only) show no settled method, and the FLoRA convergence dispute rests on a mainly-CV setup ([arXiv:2411.14961](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.14961)). The needed contribution is an independent head-to-head on FlowerTune or FedLLM-Factory that reports wall-clock time to target and bytes, not only final accuracy.

**ZO's byte savings have never been weighed against its compute and energy cost on real hardware.** FedKSeed's 18 KB/round ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/qin24a.html)) is offset by many forward passes ([Fed MobiLLM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06765)). FedSPZO still trails first-order LoRA ([arXiv:2502.10239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10239)). A crossover analysis of time and joules to accuracy against uplink bandwidth for ZO and LoRA would turn folklore into design guidance.

**Compression and privacy are studied by disjoint communities.** Low-rank or sparse updates are not private by construction: MineGrad reconstructs data from LoRA gradients even when tokens exceed the rank ([arXiv:2608.01521](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.01521)). DP noise is also amplified through the LoRA product ([LA-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19926v1); [FedASK](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09990)). How top-k error-feedback compression interacts with DP clipping and secure aggregation (top-k indices leak information and break naive SecAgg summation) is an open question for any federated, as opposed to merely decentralized, deployment (I).

**Permissionless training needs verification and incentive designs that survive.** Covenant-72B's permissionless run ([arXiv:2603.08163](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.08163)), TOPLOC's rollout verification ([arXiv:2505.07291](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07291v1)) and the reported retreats to centralized compute and off-chain governance ([implicator.ai](https://www.implicator.ai/prime-intellects-intellect-3-open-source-ambition-meets-centralized-reality/); [ownyourmind.ai](https://ownyourmind.ai/tokenomics/templar-bittensor-covenant-exit/)) all point to Byzantine-robust aggregation of *compressed* pseudo-gradients and cheap proof-of-training as unsolved. A 2026 paper already asks whether distributed training undermines compute governance ([arXiv:2605.29359](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29359)), which suggests a policy dimension that technical papers rarely address.

## Conclusion

The main change in 2025–2026 is that low-communication training stopped being a compromise. With tuned outer optimizers, DiLoCo is a *better-scaling* alternative to data parallelism, so the research question has moved from "how much quality do we lose" to "what is the minimum information that must cross the wire per unit of progress". Error feedback is the current answer. DeMo, SparseLoCo and MuLoCo converge on it from three directions, and it is what made a permissionless 72B run feasible. The next advances are likely to be theoretical (why error-feedback state can stand in for momentum, and the scaling laws that govern it) and methodological (bandwidth-parameterized time-to-target benchmarks), rather than another order of magnitude of compression, which now runs into index overheads and quality floors.

Federated fine-tuning sits on a different frontier. Bytes are mostly solved, and FedKSeed already reaches kilobytes, so speed now depends on aggregation exactness, rank heterogeneity and the compute cost of ZO. The highest-leverage open opportunity sits between the two communities: applying decentralized-pretraining machinery (outer Nesterov, error-feedback compression, gossip) to genuinely federated, non-IID, privacy-constrained data, where Photon is still nearly alone and where compression, differential privacy and secure aggregation have not yet been designed together.
